Reading a Matting Agent COA: What Each Line Means

Published: 2026-11-18 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Testing & QC

A matting agent COA (certificate of analysis) is the one-page document that travels with every lot of silica you buy. It states what was measured on that specific batch, by which method, and whether each result falls inside the agreed specification. Read properly, it tells you whether the bag on your dock will behave like the material you qualified in the lab. This guide walks through a typical COA for a silica matting agent line by line, explains what each parameter means for gloss, clarity, viscosity and stability, and gives a checklist for spotting weak or suspicious certificates.

COA, TDS and SDS: what each document is for

Buyers often receive three documents for the same product. The key difference is that a COA describes one batch, while the TDS and SDS describe the product.

DocumentScopeWhat it containsHow you use it
TDS (technical data sheet)The grade in generalTypical properties, recommended dosage, application guidanceGrade selection and formulation design
SDS (safety data sheet)The grade in generalHazard classification, handling, storage, exposure controls, transport dataPlant safety, import paperwork, regulatory files
COA (certificate of analysis)One production lotMeasured results for that lot against specification limits, with methodsIncoming inspection, lot release, traceability

A useful rule of thumb: the TDS tells you what the product should be; the COA tells you what this lot actually is. If a supplier sends a TDS when you asked for a COA, or a COA whose numbers are copied from the TDS, you do not yet have batch-level evidence. Our article on what matting agent datasheets don't tell you covers the limits of the TDS in more depth.

The header block: identity and traceability

Before looking at any numbers, check that the top of the certificate lets you trace the document to a physical lot. A good matting agent COA normally includes:

  • Product name and grade code, exactly as it appears on the bag label and your purchase order.
  • Lot or batch number, printed identically on the bags. Without a match between bag and certificate, the COA proves nothing about the goods you received.
  • Production date and, where the supplier uses one, a retest or best-before date. Moisture uptake and caking in storage are real, so lot age matters.
  • Quantity and packing covered by the certificate, and ideally your order or delivery reference.
  • Issuing laboratory, date of issue and an authorised signature or stamp, plus a statement that the lot conforms to the specification.

A shipment with several lots should come with one COA per lot.

Line by line: the test parameters on a matting agent COA

Particle size (d50, d10, d90)

This is usually the most important line on the certificate. The d50 is the median particle size: half the particles by volume are smaller, half larger. For a given dosage and film thickness, a larger d50 generally gives lower gloss because the particles create more surface roughness, while a smaller d50 gives better clarity and a smoother feel. If your COA also lists d90, watch it closely: a small shift in the coarse tail can create visible specks or a rough touch in thin films even when the d50 looks unchanged.

Particle size results depend on the instrument, optical model and sample preparation, so values from different labs are not always directly comparable. Compare lots from the same supplier measured by the same method.

Oil absorption

Oil absorption measures how much oil a given mass of powder takes up before forming a coherent paste. For porous silica it is a practical proxy for accessible pore volume. Higher oil absorption usually means more matting per kilogram but also more viscosity build, because the pores take up liquid from the formulation. A lot with noticeably higher oil absorption than your qualified material can thicken your batch and shift gloss at the same dosage. Linseed-oil rub-out and DBP absorptometer methods give different numbers, so compare like with like.

Loss on drying and loss on ignition

Loss on drying is the weight lost when the powder is heated at around 105 °C. It mostly reflects free, physically adsorbed water. Precipitated silica is hygroscopic, so this value can rise if bags are stored open or in humid warehouses. It can contribute to lumps and slower wet-out.

Loss on ignition is measured at a much higher temperature and includes chemically bound water (silanol groups condensing) plus any organic surface treatment that burns off. For wax-treated matting agents, loss on ignition, together with a carbon content line if reported, is one of the few COA indicators of how much treatment is on the silica. A significant change between lots can affect settling behaviour, surface feel and recoatability.

pH of the aqueous suspension

Silica pH is measured on a slurry, commonly around 5% in water. Values vary by grade and washing process. Silica is a small part of the formula, but an unusual lot can still nudge system pH or the efficiency of pH-sensitive thickeners, so track it from lot to lot.

Sieve residue and SiO₂ content

Sieve residue reports the fraction retained on a fine mesh after wet sieving. It catches oversized aggregates and foreign particles that laser diffraction may underweight, and it relates closely to seeds and grit in the film, so specify it tightly for clear topcoats. SiO₂ content confirms purity; it is usually stated on a dry or ignited basis, so check which basis is used before comparing suppliers.

Specification limits vs typical values

A COA should show three things for each parameter: the method, the specification limit (a range or a maximum/minimum) and the measured result for the lot. Typical values belong on the TDS. A certificate with only "typical" figures is really a data sheet.

Also compare the width of the supplier's specification with the tolerance your own process can absorb. A d50 range of ±1 μm may be fine for a primer but too wide for a water-white clear coat on dark wood, where a small shift can change clarity. If the supplier's window is wider than your process window, agree a tighter internal acceptance range for the parameters that matter most, and confirm it in writing.

Finally, keep the COAs and look at them as a series. A spreadsheet of d50, oil absorption and loss on drying across your last ten or twenty lots will show drift long before a single result goes out of specification, and gives you objective data when discussing gloss variation problems with a supplier.

Red flags when reading a COA

Most certificates are issued in good faith, but a few patterns should prompt follow-up questions:

  • No lot number, or a lot number that does not match the bag labels.
  • Results that are identical, to the last decimal, on every lot you receive.
  • Test methods not stated, or a method that changes between lots without explanation.
  • Only typical values, with no specification limits.
  • Results that sit consistently at one edge of the specification.
  • An issue date before the production date, or a COA issued long after shipment.
  • No signature, stamp or named responsible person.
  • Parameters that matter for your application (for example sieve residue for clear coats) are simply missing.

Verifying the COA in your own lab

A COA does not replace incoming inspection; it tells you where to focus it. Many coating plants run a short check on each new lot, especially in the first months with a new supplier:

  1. Match identity. Confirm grade, lot number and packing against the certificate and purchase order.
  2. Quick physical checks. Loss on drying and slurry pH need only an oven, a balance and a pH meter, and they catch storage and handling problems.
  3. Performance check against a retained standard. Disperse the new lot and your retained reference lot in the same standard formula, at the same dosage and dispersion settings, and compare Hegman fineness, viscosity and gloss at 60° and 85° on drawdowns of the same film thickness. Our guides on how to measure gloss on matte panels and dispersing silica matting agents describe the details.
  4. Record and trend. Log your results next to the supplier's COA values. If your measurements consistently disagree with the certificate, ask the supplier to compare methods.
  5. Retain a sample of every lot for a period that covers your product's shelf life, so you can investigate complaints later.

The performance check is the most important. The COA measures the powder, not the paint.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a matting agent COA?

A certificate of analysis for a matting agent is a batch-level document that reports measured results for one production lot, such as particle size, oil absorption, moisture and pH, against the agreed specification limits, with the test methods used.

2. What is the difference between a COA and a TDS?

A TDS describes the grade in general with typical values and usage guidance. A COA describes one specific lot with measured results and specification limits, and is used for incoming inspection and traceability.

3. Which COA lines matter most for gloss and clarity?

Particle size (d50 and, if reported, d90), oil absorption and sieve residue have the most direct effect on gloss, clarity and specks. Loss on drying, loss on ignition and pH matter more for handling, stability and compatibility.

4. Can I compare particle size values from different suppliers' COAs?

Only with care. Particle size results depend on the instrument, optical model and sample preparation, so compare lots measured by the same method, and confirm performance with a drawdown test in your own formula.

5. Is a COA enough to release a new lot into production?

It is a good starting point, but many plants also run a short incoming check: identity match, loss on drying, pH, and a gloss and fineness comparison against a retained reference lot in a standard formula.

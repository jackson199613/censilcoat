Matting Agents and Associative Thickeners: Avoiding Bad Interactions

Published: 2026-11-25 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Formulation

A waterborne matte clear that looked perfect on day one can lose half its Stormer viscosity in a month, separate a water layer on top, or creep up in gloss on the shelf. When that happens, the cause is often an interaction between the matting agent and the associative thickener rather than either additive alone. Porous silica has a large, reactive surface; associative thickeners build viscosity through weak, reversible links. Put them in the same can and they compete for the same surfactants, water and surfaces.

This guide explains the main interaction mechanisms, how to recognise them from symptoms, which tests separate one cause from another, and a formulation checklist that keeps the gloss target and the rheology stable together. It is written for formulators and technical buyers working with waterborne wood and plastic coatings.

How associative thickeners build viscosity

Associative thickeners are water-soluble polymers carrying hydrophobic groups. Instead of thickening only by chain entanglement, as cellulose ethers do, they form a transient network: hydrophobes cluster together, adsorb onto latex particles and join surfactant micelles. Shear breaks these junctions and they re-form when shear stops, which is why associative systems typically give good levelling and a high-shear viscosity suited to spraying and brushing.

The common families in coatings are:

  • HEUR (hydrophobically modified ethoxylated urethane): a polyethylene-glycol backbone with hydrophobic end groups. Largely pH-independent, widely used in wood clears and furniture coatings.
  • HASE (hydrophobically modified alkali-swellable emulsion): an acrylic polymer that swells and thickens when neutralised, with hydrophobes along the chain. Strongly pH-dependent.
  • HMHEC (hydrophobically modified hydroxyethyl cellulose): a cellulose backbone with hydrophobic grafts, combining entanglement and association.
  • Other non-ionic associatives such as hydrophobically modified polyethers, often used as low-shear or "sag control" partners to a HEUR.

The key point for matting is that the network depends on a delicate balance of hydrophobes, surfactant and particle surface. Anything that adds new surface or removes surfactant from the water phase can shift that balance.

Five ways a matting agent associative thickener pair can go wrong

Precipitated silica matting agents are porous particles with surface areas of a few hundred m²/g and pore volumes well above 1 mL/g. That combination drives five mechanisms that are well described in general coatings and colloid practice.

  1. Thickener adsorption on silanol groups. Polyether chains, such as the backbone of a HEUR, can hydrogen-bond to the silanol (Si–OH) groups on an untreated silica surface. Thickener that is stuck to silica is no longer available to build the network, so viscosity falls, sometimes slowly over days as adsorption reaches equilibrium.
  2. Surfactant depletion. Silica can adsorb wetting agents, emulsifiers from the binder and dispersants. HEUR viscosity is highly sensitive to free surfactant: a small amount can strengthen hydrophobe junctions, while larger amounts cap them and reduce viscosity. Removing surfactant can therefore move viscosity up or down, depending on where the formulation sits on that curve.
  3. Bridging flocculation. One thickener chain adsorbed on two or more silica particles can pull them into loose clusters. This is the interaction most likely to show up as gloss drift, seeds or a grainy appearance, because clustered silica roughens the film surface differently from well-separated particles.
  4. Depletion flocculation. The opposite case: a high concentration of thickener that does not adsorb on the silica can push particles together by an osmotic effect. It tends to appear when thickener levels are raised to compensate for a viscosity loss, and can lead to syneresis (a clear water layer) during storage.
  5. Water and co-solvent uptake into pores. Pore filling reduces the free continuous phase and can change the local concentration of co-solvents and coalescents, which themselves affect hydrophobe association. Larger thickener molecules may be partly excluded from the pores, so the effective thickener concentration in the remaining water can rise.

Tests that separate one cause from another

A single viscosity number cannot tell adsorption from surfactant effects. A short test matrix usually can. Run every test on a matched pair: the same base with and without the matting agent, made on the same day.

  • Two-shear tracking. Record Stormer KU (ASTM D562) and cone-and-plate high-shear viscosity (ASTM D4287) at 0 hours, 24 hours and after heat storage. HEUR interactions often move low and high shear in different directions.
  • Thickener ladder. Prepare the matted base at three or four thickener levels. If the viscosity loss shrinks steadily as thickener increases, adsorption on silica is likely, because the surface eventually saturates.
  • Surfactant spike. Add a small, fixed amount (for example 0.1% on total) of the formulation's own wetting agent to an aged sample. A strong viscosity response suggests the silica has shifted the surfactant balance.
  • Centrifuge or standing test. Measure the height of any clear layer after storage or a short centrifuge run to quantify syneresis.
  • Fineness and microscopy. A Hegman gauge reading (ASTM D1210) on fresh and aged paint, plus an optical microscope view of a diluted drop, shows whether silica is clustering over time.
  • Gloss on aged paint. Draw down or spray panels from both fresh and stored paint and measure 60° and 85° gloss. A shift with the same film thickness points to flocculation rather than a change in dosage.

Accelerated storage at 40–50 °C for several weeks is a common way to bring slow interactions forward. Confirm conclusions with ambient-storage samples, because heat can also change binder and thickener behaviour on its own.

Formulation checklist to keep the pair stable

  1. Wet and disperse the silica first. Add the matting agent into water, wetting agent and part of the binder under high-speed stirring, then let it rest before thickening. Our note on dispersing matting agents without bead milling covers the routine.
  2. Add the associative thickener last, pre-diluted. Dilute HEUR in water (and co-solvent if the supplier recommends it) and add under moderate stirring. Avoid pouring neat thickener onto freshly wetted silica.
  3. Fix the final thickener level after 24 hours. Adjust against the equilibrated value, not the value measured straight after dispersion.
  4. Keep the surfactant package lean and stable. Count every source of surfactant, including the binder and colourant, and avoid large late additions that can collapse HEUR viscosity.
  5. Use a balanced thickener combination. Pairing a high-shear HEUR with a low-shear associative or a small amount of entanglement thickener is often more robust than pushing one product to its limit.
  6. Control pH in HASE systems. Check pH after silica addition and after storage, and choose a neutraliser that does not evaporate quickly if pH drift is a problem.
  7. Prefer surface-treated silica where interactions are strong. A wax-treated surface covers part of the silanol area that adsorbs polyethers and surfactants, which generally reduces both thickener loss and bridging.
  8. Re-qualify the thickener level whenever the matting grade changes. Two grades with similar gloss can differ in surface chemistry and pore structure. Substitution projects, such as replacing an imported grade covered on our OK 412 alternative page, should include a thickener ladder, not only a gloss check.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why does my HEUR-thickened matte coating lose viscosity after adding silica?

The two most common reasons are adsorption of the thickener's polyether backbone onto silanol groups on the silica surface, which removes thickener from the network, and a shift in the free surfactant level, which changes how HEUR hydrophobes associate. A simple ladder test with extra thickener or a small surfactant addition helps tell the two apart.

2. Should the matting agent go in before or after the associative thickener?

In most waterborne clears it is safer to wet and disperse the matting agent first, let the batch equilibrate, and add the associative thickener last as a diluted solution under moderate stirring. Adding concentrated thickener onto dry or freshly wetted silica gives the most adsorption and local gelling.

3. Can an associative thickener cause silica flocculation and gloss drift?

It can. Thickener chains that bridge several silica particles, or a large excess of non-adsorbing thickener that causes depletion flocculation, can both cluster the matting agent. Clusters change surface roughness, so gloss and sheen uniformity may drift during storage and seeds can appear on the fineness gauge.

4. Do wax-treated matting agents interact less with associative thickeners?

Generally they interact less, because the wax layer covers part of the silanol surface that would otherwise hydrogen-bond with polyether segments and adsorb surfactant. The effect still depends on the specific thickener and surfactant package, so a matched lab comparison is recommended.

5. How long should I store samples before judging the interaction?

Measure at 0 hours, after 24 hours and after an accelerated heat-storage cycle, for example several weeks at 40 to 50 °C. Many interactions develop slowly, so the initial reading alone can be misleading.

High-Speed Disperser Settings for Matting Agents: Tip Speed and Time

Published: 2026-11-17 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Formulation

The high speed disperser tip speed you choose, together with how long you run, decides whether a silica matting agent ends up as individual particles doing their job at the film surface, or as a mix of seeds, foam and drifting gloss. Most matting grades need far less energy than a pigment grind: a moderate tip speed for a fixed, recorded time is usually enough. The difficulty is that "speed 3 for a while" does not transfer between a 50 mm lab blade and a 400 mm production blade. This article gives the formula, worked numbers, typical starting windows for each stage, how to set dispersion time, and how to scale settings from the lab to the plant.

It assumes you already know why matting silica is dispersed rather than milled. If not, start with our overview of dispersing silica matting agents without bead milling, then come back here for the numbers.

What tip speed is and how to calculate it

Tip speed is the linear velocity of the outer edge of the disperser blade. It is the single most useful number for describing how hard a saw-tooth blade is working, because the shear zone that wets and deagglomerates powder sits right at that edge.

Tip speed (m/s) = π × blade diameter (m) × rpm ÷ 60

Turned around, the shaft speed needed for a target tip speed is rpm = 60 × tip speed ÷ (π × blade diameter). Imperial specifications are often given in feet per minute; 1 m/s is about 197 ft/min.

The table shows the trap. A lab operator who runs "3,000 rpm" on a 50 mm blade is at about 7.9 m/s. Copy "3,000 rpm" to a 100 mm pilot blade and the tip speed doubles to about 15.7 m/s. The opposite happens in production: a large blade turning a few hundred rpm can be working just as hard as a small lab blade spinning at several thousand. Write batch instructions in m/s (or ft/min), and give the rpm for each machine as a derived value.

Typical tip speed windows by process stage

A matting batch passes through stages that need different energy. Running the whole batch at one speed tends to cause either dusting and lumps at the start, or foam and heat at the end. The ranges below are general industry starting points for saw-tooth dispersers in waterborne coatings, not universal rules. Confirm them in your own base with the time study described later.

StageTypical tip speedGoalWhat to watch
Powder incorporationLow to moderate, ~4–8 m/s, rising as the batch thickensDraw powder into the vortex without dusting or floating raftsAdd gradually; keep a rolling flow with the blade just visible
Deagglomeration of matting silicaModerate, often ~10–18 m/sBreak soft agglomerates; complete wetting of poresFoam, temperature rise, gloss versus standard
Pigment grind (for comparison)Higher, often high teens to mid-20s m/sDevelop colour strength and finenessNot usually needed for matting silica alone
Binder letdown and additionsLow, ~3–6 m/sMix without shear damage or air entrainmentResin shock, coalescent addition rate, foam

Two points are worth stressing. First, higher is not better for matting silica: once agglomerates are gone, extra energy only heats the batch, entrains air and, in the extreme, damages surface treatment and lowers matting efficiency. Second, the deagglomeration window you settle on should be the lowest tip speed that consistently reaches your fineness and gloss targets in the set time. That gives you headroom when a batch is slightly thicker or colder than normal.

Dispersion time: when the clock starts and how to set it

Tip speed and time work together. A useful way to think about it is energy input: power drawn by the motor multiplied by time, divided by batch mass. Doubling time at the same speed roughly doubles the energy, and so does a large increase in viscosity at the same speed. That is why a time instruction without a tip speed, or a tip speed without a viscosity range, is incomplete.

Define the start point

The most common source of batch-to-batch variation is ambiguity about when dispersion time begins. Write it down explicitly: the clock starts when the last of the matting agent has been incorporated and the disperser has reached the set tip speed. Time spent adding powder does not count, because during incorporation much of the silica has not yet reached the shear zone.

Run a time study

  1. Make a single base batch and split it into five equal portions at the same matting agent level.
  2. Disperse each portion at one fixed tip speed for 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 minutes.
  3. Record the batch temperature at the end of each run.
  4. Read fineness (Hegman, ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210) and viscosity at a fixed time after dispersion.
  5. Draw down at the target film thickness and measure 60° and 85° gloss after full cure.
  6. Plot gloss against time. Choose the point where gloss and fineness have levelled off, and check that the 30-minute sample has not drifted back up.

If time permits, repeat at a second tip speed a few m/s higher or lower. Two curves tell you how forgiving the formula is. A broad, flat plateau means operator variation will hardly affect gloss; a steep curve means the instruction must be followed tightly. For reliable gloss readings on low-sheen panels, see how to measure gloss correctly on matte panels.

Geometry and viscosity: why the same tip speed behaves differently

Tip speed only describes the blade. Whether that energy reaches the powder depends on the flow pattern in the vessel, which is governed by geometry and viscosity. Common guidelines from dispersion practice are:

  • Vessel diameter about 2–3 times the blade diameter. Larger vessels leave dead zones near the wall.
  • Blade height about 0.5–1 blade diameter above the vessel bottom.
  • Liquid depth about 1–2 blade diameters. Shallow batches pull air into the vortex; deep batches leave the upper layer stagnant.
  • Flow pattern: a rolling "doughnut" with a small vortex where the blade is just visible. A deep, open vortex means air is being whipped in; a flat, motionless surface means the material is not turning over.

Viscosity matters as much as speed. In a very thin base, the blade slips through the liquid; energy turns into turbulence and foam rather than shear on the agglomerates. In a very thick base, the batch moves only near the blade and the rest of the vessel stalls. Many plants therefore disperse matting silica at a moderate working viscosity, for example in the grind stage with part of the binder or a rheology additive present, and add the main thickener after the silica is fully wetted. Porous silica keeps taking up liquid for some minutes, so viscosity readings taken immediately after dispersion will be lower than readings taken later; always measure at a fixed interval.

Scaling high speed disperser tip speed from lab to production

Scale-up is where most settings go wrong. Keeping tip speed constant is the right first step, but it is not the whole story.

FactorWhat changes at larger scaleHow to handle it
Tip speedSame m/s needs much lower rpm on a larger bladeSpecify m/s; list derived rpm per machine
Geometry ratiosProduction tanks are often wider or fuller than the lab equivalentKeep vessel/blade, height and depth ratios close to the lab set-up
Batch turnoverLower rpm means fewer passes of material through the shear zone per minuteExpect to verify, and sometimes extend, dispersion time in the first production batches
TemperatureLarge batches lose heat more slowly and can run warmerLog start and end temperature; set a maximum based on binder supplier guidance
Powder additionBags go in faster than a lab spatula, creating dry lumpsSpecify addition rate or time; use a sifting screen or powder induction where available

A practical approach is to treat the first two or three production batches as confirmation runs: take samples at intervals during dispersion, check fineness and drawdown gloss against the lab standard, and fix the production time from those results. Recording motor power or current alongside time gives a further reference, since energy input per kilogram tends to transfer more faithfully than time alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a good high speed disperser tip speed for silica matting agents?

As a general starting point, many waterborne formulators deagglomerate matting silica at a moderate tip speed, often around 10–18 m/s, which is lower than a typical pigment grind. Use the lowest tip speed that consistently reaches your fineness and gloss targets in the set time.

2. How do I convert rpm to tip speed?

Tip speed in m/s equals π × blade diameter in metres × rpm ÷ 60. A 50 mm blade at 3,000 rpm runs at about 7.9 m/s; a 300 mm blade reaches the same speed at only about 500 rpm.

3. How long should I disperse a matting agent?

It depends on the grade, tip speed and base viscosity. Many matting grades need 15–20 minutes. Start the clock only after all powder is incorporated and the set tip speed is reached, and confirm the time with a 5–30 minute time study.

4. Why does gloss change when I scale up from lab to production?

Usually because rpm was copied instead of tip speed, geometry ratios differ, or the larger batch turns over more slowly through the shear zone. Keep tip speed and geometry constant and confirm dispersion time on the first production batches.

5. Can I disperse matting silica at too high a tip speed?

Yes. After agglomerates are broken, extra energy mainly adds heat and air, and prolonged high shear can degrade particles or surface treatment so gloss rises. Cap both tip speed and time in the batch instruction.

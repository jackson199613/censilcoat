How Matting Agents Change Viscosity and Rheology

Published: 2026-11-21 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Formulation

Adding a silica matting agent to a waterborne coating almost always makes it thicker, and the change is rarely a simple, linear rise. The same 3% of silica can leave a clear lacquer sprayable at one shop and push it into a gel-like, hard-to-atomise state at another. Understanding matting agent viscosity and rheology effects lets you predict that shift, measure it at the shear rates that matter, and correct it without sacrificing the gloss target.

This guide is for formulators and technical buyers working on waterborne wood and plastic coatings. It explains why porous silica changes flow behaviour, which measurements to run, how thickeners and silica interact, and a step-by-step way to rebalance rheology after matting.

Matting agent viscosity and rheology: the four mechanisms

Precipitated silica matting agents are porous, high-surface-area particles. Four mechanisms, acting together, explain most of the viscosity change you see after adding them.

  1. Liquid uptake into pores. A typical matting silica has a pore volume well above 1 mL/g. When it is wetted, part of the water, co-solvent and dissolved additives moves into the pores. That liquid no longer acts as free continuous phase, so the remaining paint behaves as if it had a higher solids content.
  2. Effective volume fraction. Because a porous particle carries its pore liquid with it, its hydrodynamic volume is much larger than its skeletal (solid silica) volume. Viscosity in dispersions rises steeply, not linearly, as the effective volume fraction of particles approaches packing limits. This is why a small step from 3% to 4% silica can have a bigger effect than the step from 1% to 2%.
  3. Surface interactions. Silica surfaces carry silanol (Si–OH) groups that can hydrogen-bond with each other and with binder, surfactants and thickeners. In some systems this builds a weak particle network that gives shear-thinning and time-dependent (thixotropic) behaviour. Surface treatments such as wax coating reduce this interaction.
  4. Adsorption of additives. With a surface area in the hundreds of m²/g, the silica can adsorb wetting agents, dispersants and the hydrophobic segments of associative thickeners. Removing these from the liquid phase changes how the thickener network forms, which can raise or lower viscosity depending on the system.

The first two mechanisms mostly raise viscosity across all shear rates. The last two mainly change the shape of the flow curve, which is why low-shear and high-shear readings often move by very different amounts.

Which shear rates matter in a matte coating

A single Brookfield number tells you little about how a matte coating will spray, level or store. Paint experiences very different shear rates during its life. The values below are typical orders of magnitude used in general coatings practice:

ProcessApproximate shear rate (s⁻¹)What silica can change
Storage and settlingBelow 0.1Low-shear viscosity that slows sedimentation; hard or soft sediment
Levelling and saggingAbout 0.01–10Recovery after application, orange peel versus sag on vertical parts
Stirring, pumping, can feelAbout 10–1,000Mid-shear viscosity (Stormer KU), ease of mixing
Brushing and rollingAbout 1,000–10,000Brush drag and film build per coat
SprayingAbout 1,000–100,000 and aboveAtomisation quality, droplet size, overspray texture

Matting silica often raises low-shear viscosity proportionally more than high-shear viscosity. That can be helpful (better anti-settling and sag resistance) or harmful (poor levelling, visible spray texture, uneven sheen). The goal is to know where the change happens before you decide how to correct it.

How to measure the rheology change

Use a matched pair: the same base with and without matting agent, made the same day and measured at the same temperature (usually 23 or 25 °C). Common methods include:

  • Rotational viscometer (Brookfield type, ASTM D2196). Measure at two or three spindle speeds, not one. The ratio of low-speed to high-speed readings gives a simple shear-thinning index.
  • Stormer viscometer (ASTM D562). Krebs units describe mid-shear consistency and are widely used for architectural and wood coatings.
  • Cone-and-plate high-shear viscometer (ASTM D4287). An ICI-type reading at around 10,000 s⁻¹ relates to brush drag and, roughly, to spray behaviour.
  • Flow cups (ISO 2431, ASTM D1200). Quick and useful for production control of spray viscosity, but they cannot separate shear-thinning from Newtonian behaviour.
  • Rotational rheometer. A full flow curve plus a three-interval thixotropy test (low shear, high shear, low shear) shows how fast viscosity recovers after application, which links directly to levelling and sag.
  • Sag resistance (ASTM D4400). An anti-sag applicator is a practical check that complements rheometer data.

Time matters. Liquid uptake into pores and additive adsorption are not instantaneous. Measure immediately after dispersion, again after 24 hours, and after a heat-storage cycle. Many "viscosity spikes" reported from production are simply the 24-hour value that the lab never measured.

Silica and thickeners: avoiding bad interactions

Most waterborne wood and plastic coatings use one or more of three thickener families. Each reacts differently to matting silica, and the behaviour depends strongly on the specific products used, so treat the points below as general starting guidance.

  • HEUR (hydrophobically modified ethoxylated urethane). These build viscosity through associations between hydrophobic end groups, latex particles and surfactant micelles. Silica that adsorbs surfactant can shift that balance. Watch both low-shear (KU) and high-shear (ICI) values, as they may move in opposite directions.
  • HASE / ASE (alkali-swellable acrylics). These are pH-dependent and generally give strong low-shear structure. Check pH after silica addition and after storage, since a pH drift can change thickening efficiency.
  • Cellulosic thickeners (e.g. HEC). These thicken mainly by chain entanglement and are less sensitive to adsorption, but they increase low-shear viscosity strongly and can worsen levelling in matte clears.

Two practical rules help in nearly every system. First, add and fully disperse the matting agent before making the final thickener adjustment, so that you are correcting the real viscosity rather than a temporary one. Second, if you change matting grade, re-check the thickener level rather than assuming the old ratio still applies.

A step-by-step rebalancing routine

  1. Fix the gloss target first. Run a 1/2/3/4% dosage ladder and pick the lowest level that meets gloss at nominal and ±5 μm dry film thickness.
  2. Measure the matted base without further adjustment. Record low-, mid- and high-shear values at 0 and 24 hours.
  3. Compare against the unmatted base. Decide which part of the flow curve moved. A low-shear-only rise often needs less thickener; a rise across all shear rates usually needs a water or solids adjustment.
  4. Adjust thickener and water in small steps. Keep a written log of each change and re-measure after equilibration.
  5. Apply, then confirm appearance. Spray or brush panels and check gloss, levelling, sag and sheen uniformity. Rheology numbers are only useful if they predict the finished film.
  6. Run heat storage. Store the adjusted paint warm (for example 40–50 °C) for several weeks and re-check viscosity, sediment and gloss. Our article on eliminating hard sediment covers the settling side in more detail.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why does adding a matting agent increase viscosity?

Porous precipitated silica absorbs part of the liquid phase into its pores, raising the effective volume fraction of particles. Its large surface can also adsorb surfactants and thickener segments. Together these usually raise viscosity, often more at low shear than at high shear.

2. When should I measure viscosity after adding silica?

Measure immediately after dispersion, again after 24 hours, and after a heat-storage cycle. Pore filling and additive adsorption take time, so the 24-hour value is the one to use for final thickener adjustment.

3. Do finer matting agents thicken more than coarser ones?

Often, yes. At equal dosage a finer grade adds more particles and more external surface, which tends to raise viscosity, while giving less matting per gram. Coarser grades reach low gloss at lower dosage but can feel rough on thin films.

4. Should I cut the matting agent to fix high viscosity?

Usually not as a first step, because gloss will rise. Fix the gloss target first, then rebalance thickener and water. If viscosity is still too high, a more efficient grade that reaches the same gloss at lower dosage is a better route.

5. Which viscosity tests are most useful for matte waterborne coatings?

A combination works best: Stormer KU (ASTM D562) for mid-shear consistency, cone-and-plate ICI (ASTM D4287) for high shear, a multi-speed rotational viscometer (ASTM D2196) for shear thinning, and a sag test (ASTM D4400). A rheometer thixotropy test adds levelling insight.

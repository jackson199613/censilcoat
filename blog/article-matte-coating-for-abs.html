Matte Waterborne Coatings for ABS Parts

Published: 2026-11-21 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Plastic coatings

A matte coating for ABS has to do three things at once: hit a tight gloss window, stick to a smooth moulded plastic, and survive handling, cleaning agents and heat without polishing up or cracking the part. Waterborne systems can do all three, but ABS is less forgiving than wood or metal. The plastic is sensitive to some solvents and coalescents, it cannot take high bake temperatures, and the films applied to it are often thin, which narrows the choice of matting agent.

This guide is for formulators and technical buyers working on waterborne matte paints for ABS housings, appliance panels, automotive interior trim, toys and consumer goods. It covers substrate preparation, binder choice, how to select and dose a silica matting agent for thin films, a practical test plan and a troubleshooting table.

Why ABS is a demanding substrate for matte waterborne coatings

ABS (acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene) is popular because it moulds well, takes colour and is tough at a reasonable cost. The same properties create specific coating challenges:

  • Moderate surface energy and smooth tooling surfaces. ABS is easier to wet than polyolefins, so it rarely needs aggressive pre-treatment, but highly polished moulds give a very smooth surface with little mechanical key. Adhesion depends on the binder wetting and interacting with the plastic.
  • Environmental stress cracking. Moulded ABS parts carry residual stress, especially around bosses, clips and gates. Certain solvents and coalescing agents can attack stressed areas and cause fine cracks, sometimes days after painting. This is one reason waterborne systems are attractive, but the coalescent package still needs care.
  • Limited heat tolerance. General-purpose ABS softens well below 100 °C, so force-drying is usually kept moderate. Lower drying temperatures mean slower water release and coalescence, which affects how the matting agent ends up distributed in the film.
  • Thin films. Decorative and functional topcoats on ABS are commonly applied at roughly 10–30 μm dry. In thin films, a matting particle that is large relative to the film protrudes too far and feels gritty; one that is too small cannot create enough surface roughness to reach low gloss.
  • Contaminants from moulding. Release agents, antistatic additives and handling residues can migrate to the surface and cause poor wetting, craters and adhesion loss that is easily mistaken for a matting problem.

Surface preparation before you think about gloss

Many "matting" complaints on ABS turn out to be surface problems. Settle the substrate first so that later trials only change one variable.

  1. Know the moulding history. Ask whether the moulder uses external release agents or internal lubricants. Silicone-based release agents are a common cause of fisheyes and poor adhesion.
  2. Clean consistently. A wipe with a suitable cleaner (often an isopropyl alcohol/water blend) or a power wash line removes dust and fingerprints. Check that the cleaner itself does not attack the ABS grade in use.
  3. Control static. Plastic parts attract dust that shows clearly on dark matte finishes. Ionised air blow-off before spraying is common practice.
  4. Consider surface activation for difficult parts. Flame or plasma treatment is rarely essential for ABS but can widen the adhesion window for blends or heavily lubricated grades.
  5. Test adhesion of the unmatted clear or base. If the binder alone fails cross-hatch, no matting agent will fix it.

Binder systems that work on ABS

The matting agent sets the gloss, but the binder decides adhesion, hardness and chemical resistance. The table below summarises general formulation practice for waterborne systems on ABS:

Whichever binder you choose, screen coalescents on stressed test parts, not only on flat plaques. A simple check is to coat parts with bosses and clips, age them for a few days and inspect under magnification for crazing.

Choosing a matting agent for a matte coating on ABS

Silica matting agents lower gloss by creating micro-roughness at the film surface as the coating shrinks during drying. On ABS, the key selection criteria are particle size relative to film thickness, matting efficiency, surface treatment and stability in the can.

Particle size versus dry film thickness

As a general rule, choose a d50 well below the dry film thickness and check the upper end of the distribution (d90 or the full curve), because a small fraction of oversize particles is what customers feel as grit. For a 15–20 μm topcoat, fine and medium grades in the 5–7 μm d50 range are the usual starting point. Coarser grades reach low gloss at lower dosage, but on thin films they can make the surface feel sandy and show texture on dark colours.

Matting efficiency and dosage

Porous precipitated silica mats efficiently because its high pore volume gives a lot of particle volume per gram. That matters on ABS because every extra percent of silica also raises viscosity, can reduce flexibility and, at high loadings, can weaken adhesion and intercoat performance. Aim for the lowest dose that holds gloss across the realistic film thickness range.

Surface treatment

Wax-treated silica helps in two ways: it gives soft, stir-back sediment rather than hard cake in low-viscosity waterborne paints, and it tends to improve scratch and burnish resistance on handled parts. Untreated grades can work well in some systems but deserve extra attention to storage stability.

Formulation and application tips

  • Run a dosage ladder. Prepare the same base at 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% matting agent on total formulation. Spray at nominal film build and at about ±5 μm, and plot gloss against dosage for each thickness.
  • Disperse, do not grind. Add silica gradually into the liquid phase and disperse at high speed for a fixed time. Over-shearing reduces particle size and matting efficiency. Our note on dispersing matting agents without bead milling covers a practical routine.
  • Adjust rheology after matting. Porous silica absorbs liquid and thickens the paint. Correct spray viscosity with the thickener and water balance rather than cutting the matting level.
  • Keep drying consistent. Slow, humid flash-off lets the binder flow over particles and raises gloss; aggressive airflow can freeze in uneven sheen. Record temperature, humidity and airflow in every trial, and stay within the heat limit of the ABS grade.
  • Filter before spraying. A filter mesh suited to the particle size catches agglomerates that would show as seeds on smooth housings.
  • For 2K systems, measure gloss on panels sprayed at the start and end of pot life, because gloss can drift as the mix ages.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the best matting agent for a matte coating on ABS?

For most waterborne matte topcoats on ABS, a wax-treated precipitated silica with a d50 well below the dry film thickness is the usual starting point. Medium grades around 6 μm suit matte and deep-matte targets; finer grades suit thinner films and smoother feel.

2. How much matting agent should a waterborne ABS coating contain?

It depends on binder, film build and gloss target. Censilcoat grades are used at 1–4% on total formulation. Run a 1/2/3/4% ladder at nominal and ±5 μm film thickness and choose the lowest dose that holds gloss.

3. Why does my matte paint lose adhesion on ABS?

Common causes are release agents or other surface contamination, a binder not suited to ABS, or a matting level so high that binder contact is reduced. Test the unmatted binder first, clean parts consistently, then add the matting agent.

4. Can a waterborne coating cause stress cracking on ABS?

Yes, if the coalescent or co-solvent package attacks stressed areas of the moulding. Screen coalescents on real parts with bosses and clips, age them and inspect for crazing before approving the formulation.

5. How do I measure gloss on matte ABS parts?

Measure 60° gloss per ISO 2813 or ASTM D523 on flat areas, and add 85° readings for deep-matte finishes, where 60° values become small and less discriminating.

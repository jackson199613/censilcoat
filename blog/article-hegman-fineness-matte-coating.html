Hegman Fineness for Matte Coatings: What Reading Is Right?

Published: 2026-11-26 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Testing & QC

Every QC lab that makes matte coatings has a fineness gauge on the bench, and almost every lab has argued at some point about what the reading should be. The short answer to "what Hegman fineness is right for a matte coating?" is that it is not the same number you would use for a gloss enamel. A matte film needs its silica particles to stay several micrometres in size, so a matte batch will never grind down to a high-gloss reading, and should not. The right specification is a range set against your own approved standard, typically read on a 0–50 μm gauge, with a clear rule for what counts as a reject: coarse agglomerates, grit and undispersed lumps, not the matting particles you added on purpose.

What a fineness-of-grind gauge measures

A grind gauge is a hardened steel block with one or two tapered channels, deepest at one end and running out to zero at the other. A small amount of paint is placed at the deep end and drawn down the channel with a flat scraper. Wherever a particle or agglomerate is larger than the local channel depth, it cannot pass under the scraper and shows up as a speck or a scratch in the film. The depth at which those specks first appear in a defined pattern is the fineness reading.

Two standards describe the method. ASTM D1210, fineness of dispersion of pigment-vehicle systems by Hegman-type gage, is widely used in North America and Asia, while ISO 1524, determination of fineness of grind for paints, varnishes and printing inks, is the international equivalent and reports results in micrometres. The principle is the same; the details of gauge ranges and reading rules differ, so a specification should always state which method it follows.

The most important point for matte coatings is that a grind gauge does not measure average particle size. It detects the largest particles present in meaningful numbers. A silica with a d50 of 5 μm still contains a coarse tail, and the gauge reports where that tail and any agglomerates sit, not where the median is. This is why a fineness reading can never be compared directly with the d50 on a datasheet.

Hegman units, micrometres and other scales

The Hegman scale runs from 0 to 8. Zero Hegman corresponds to a channel depth of 100 μm (4 mils), and 8 Hegman corresponds to zero depth. Each Hegman unit is therefore 12.5 μm.

For matte coatings, a 0–50 μm gauge is usually the best choice, because it spreads the useful range across the full length of the channel and makes differences of a few micrometres easier to see. A 0–100 μm gauge compresses that range into the shallow half and makes readings less precise.

Why matte coatings read coarser than gloss coatings

In a gloss enamel, everything that shows on the gauge is a defect: pigment agglomerates that should have been broken down. In a Hegman fineness matte coating test, part of what you see is the matting agent itself. Silica matting agents work because particles of several micrometres sit near the film surface and create micro-roughness that scatters light. Precipitated silica for waterborne coatings commonly has a d50 in the 4–9 μm range, and its largest particles extend well above that.

So a matte batch has two populations on the gauge:

  • Intended matting particles. These produce a fine, even, sand-like texture in the drawdown, starting at a depth related to the coarse end of the silica's particle size distribution. They are supposed to be there.
  • Defects. Undispersed silica lumps, dried skin, gel particles from the binder, foreign grit and pigment agglomerates. These appear as isolated larger specks or streaks further toward the deep end of the gauge, or as scratches running down the channel.

A matte specification must accept the first population and reject the second. Chasing a high-gloss reading by extending dispersion or switching to a bead mill is a common mistake. Precipitated silica particles are porous aggregates, and excessive shear can break them down. That reduces matting efficiency, so gloss rises and more silica is needed to hit target, which in turn raises viscosity and cost. How to read a grind gauge consistently

Fineness readings are notorious for operator-to-operator variation. Most of it comes from technique, not from the paint. A practical routine that follows the spirit of the standards:

  1. Clean and level the gauge. Remove all dried paint from the channel and place the gauge on a flat, non-slip surface.
  2. Sample correctly. Stir the batch gently before sampling. Avoid foam and surface skin; bubbles and skin fragments show up as false specks.
  3. Apply excess paint at the deep end. Place enough material to fill the channel slightly beyond the deepest mark.
  4. Draw down steadily. Hold the scraper with both hands, perpendicular to the gauge and close to vertical, and pull it toward the zero end in one smooth movement lasting one to two seconds with firm, even pressure.
  5. Read immediately. View the gauge at a low angle against good light, within a few seconds of the drawdown. Waterborne coatings dry quickly in the shallow end and the film can change appearance as it flashes off, so a delayed reading drifts.
  6. Apply a consistent reading rule. Read the point where particles first appear as a definite pattern or band across the channel, and record isolated coarse specks or scratches separately. ISO 1524 and ASTM D1210 each define how to treat scattered particles, so use the rule from the method your specification cites.
  7. Repeat. Make at least two drawdowns and report the result according to the method, rather than picking the better one.

Low-viscosity waterborne clears can level before the pattern is visible, so read them quickly and treat every sample the same way.

What reading is right for your matte coating?

There is no universal Hegman number for matte coatings, because the right value depends on the matting agent, the gloss target and the dry film thickness. The following ranges reflect common industry practice and are meant as a starting point, not a requirement:

Two practical rules make the reading meaningful:

  • The reading must sit well below the dry film thickness for the finish to feel smooth. Thin plastic topcoats of 10–15 μm dry film are the most sensitive: a particle much larger than the film thickness protrudes as a visible seed. Thicker wood topcoats tolerate a slightly coarser grind.
  • Set the specification from your own good batches. Record the fineness of five to ten batches that passed gloss, appearance and touch, then set the limit just outside that spread. A specification copied from a gloss product or from a competitor's datasheet rarely fits.

Using fineness as a QC tool, not just a number

The grind gauge is most useful as an early warning that something in dispersion has changed. Typical patterns and their likely causes:

  • Reading coarser than usual, gloss lower than usual: dispersion time too short, low tip speed or silica added too fast and not fully wetted. Check disperser settings; our guide to high-speed disperser tip speed and time covers the typical ranges.
  • Reading finer than usual, gloss higher than usual: over-dispersion or extended milling breaking down silica. Matting efficiency has been lost.
  • Normal band but isolated large specks: agglomerates from damp or compacted bags, dried paint from equipment, binder gel or dirt. Filter the batch and check raw material storage.
  • Scratches or streaks down the channel: hard grit or foreign particles. Check the cleanliness of tanks, lids and the filter mesh.
  • Reading changes after storage: flocculation or agglomeration in the can. Compare fineness and gloss on the fresh batch and after heat-aged storage.

Always read the gauge together with a gloss reading on a standard drawdown. Fineness alone cannot tell you whether a batch is right; the pair of numbers usually can. Our matting agent troubleshooting guide covers related defects such as specks, caking and viscosity spikes.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Hegman fineness?

Hegman fineness is a reading taken on a grind gauge that shows the size of the largest particles or agglomerates in a liquid coating. The Hegman scale runs from 0 to 8, where 0 corresponds to 100 μm and 8 to 0 μm, so each unit equals 12.5 μm. The method is described in ASTM D1210 and, in micrometres, in ISO 1524.

2. What Hegman reading is typical for a matte coating?

There is no single correct value. In general practice, many matte waterborne topcoats read roughly in the 20–30 μm region (about Hegman 5.5–6.5), with high-clarity clears finer and deep-matte plastic finishes coarser. The right limit should be set from your own approved batches and the dry film thickness of the coating.

3. Why can't a matte coating reach the same fineness as a gloss coating?

Matting works because silica particles of several micrometres create micro-roughness at the film surface. Those particles are meant to be there and show on the gauge. Grinding them down to a gloss-level reading breaks the porous silica, lowers matting efficiency and raises gloss.

4. Is the Hegman reading the same as the d50 of the matting agent?

No. A grind gauge detects the largest particles present in meaningful numbers, not the median. A silica with a d50 of about 5 μm will usually give a gauge reading several times larger, because the coarse tail of the distribution and any agglomerates set the reading.

5. Which gauge range should I use for matte coatings?

A 0–50 μm gauge is usually the most practical, because it spreads the relevant range over the whole channel and makes small differences easier to see. A 0–100 μm gauge is less precise in this range, and very fine gauges are designed for inks and high-gloss systems.

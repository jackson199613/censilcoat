Indonesia Wood Coatings: The Shift Toward Waterborne

Published: 2026-11-23 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Market & regulation

Interest in Indonesia wood coatings that are waterborne has grown steadily as Indonesian furniture makers chase export buyers who ask for lower-VOC finishes, documented supply chains and consistent natural-wood looks. The shift is real but uneven: many factories still finish with nitrocellulose, solvent-borne polyurethane or acid-curing lacquers, while export-focused plants add waterborne lines product by product. For coating formulators and raw-material buyers, the move to water changes what matters in every additive, and the matting agent is one of the first places problems show up. This article explains what is driving the shift in Indonesia, the technical hurdles of waterborne matte finishes in a hot and humid climate, and a practical checklist for selecting and qualifying silica matting agents.

It is written for R&D managers at coating plants that supply furniture makers in Central and East Java, Jepara, Cirebon and the Jakarta region, and for the procurement teams who buy their raw materials.

Indonesia's furniture industry and its coating demand

Indonesia is a major producer of solid-wood and rattan furniture. According to the Ministry of Trade, furniture and handicraft exports reached approximately USD 2.22 billion between January and November 2024, and the Indonesian Furniture and Craft Industry Association (HIMKI) has reiterated a target of USD 6 billion in exports within the next five years, as reported by RRI / Voice of Indonesia in March 2026. The same report notes that the United States and Europe dominate today's export markets, with the Middle East and Africa named as growth targets.

Three features of the industry shape coating demand:

  • Solid wood is central. Plantation teak, mahogany, mindi, acacia and rubberwood are widely used, alongside rattan and engineered panels. Open-grain species such as teak and mahogany are often finished to show the grain, which puts clarity and depth at the top of the requirement list.
  • Clusters of small and mid-size workshops. Centres such as Jepara for carved solid-wood furniture and Cirebon for rattan contain many smaller producers alongside large export factories. Finishing practice varies widely, from hand spraying in open sheds to automated spray lines with drying tunnels.
  • Export buyers set the specification. Brand owners usually approve a finish against a master panel and expect it to repeat across orders. Increasingly they also specify VOC limits, restricted-substance lists and documentation.

Why Indonesia wood coatings are moving to waterborne

No single law has flipped the market. The shift toward waterborne Indonesia wood coatings comes from several pressures acting together.

1. Buyer and brand requirements

Retailers and brands in Europe, North America and other markets increasingly ask for low-VOC finishes and declarations on substances of concern. A furniture maker exporting under several brands often finds it simpler to standardise on finishes that meet the strictest buyer's list. Waterborne acrylic and polyurethane dispersion systems are one of the main routes to low VOC content.

2. Supply-chain scrutiny of wood products

Indonesia operates the SVLK timber legality assurance system, and since November 2016 its verified exports to the EU have been eligible for FLEGT licences, a development covered by Eco-Business. On top of that, the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) adds due-diligence and geolocation requirements for wood products placed on the EU market; its application date has been postponed to 30 December 2026, as summarised by EY. Dates and scope have changed more than once, so confirm the current position with your trade advisers. These rules concern the wood rather than the coating, but they raise the general level of documentation that buyers expect from every supplier in the chain, including coating plants and their raw-material sources.

3. Worker health and factory conditions

Spraying solvent-borne lacquers in semi-open workshops exposes workers to solvent vapour and creates fire risk. Waterborne systems reduce both, which matters to factory owners and to buyers who run social-compliance audits.

4. Design trends toward natural, low-sheen surfaces

Matte and semi-matte finishes that keep teak and other woods looking close to raw remain popular. That pushes gloss targets down to the 10–30 GU range at 60° for many export programmes, which makes the matting agent a bigger part of the formulation challenge.

What to look for in a matting agent for Indonesian waterborne wood coatings

Silica matting agents create a micro-rough surface as the film shrinks during drying, scattering light and lowering gloss. In a waterborne wood coating the right grade has to do this without clouding the film, thickening the system or settling hard. The properties below carry most of the weight.

  • Particle size (d50) and top size. Coarser particles matt more efficiently but reduce clarity and feel rougher; finer particles give smoother touch and better transparency. For clear finishes over teak, a fine, tightly distributed grade usually wins. Oversized particles cause seeds and grit, so check top size or Hegman fineness, not only the median.
  • Pore volume. Higher pore volume generally means more matting per kilogram, because more particles are present at the same dosage. It also absorbs more liquid, which affects viscosity.
  • Surface treatment. Wax-treated silica tends to form soft, easily re-stirred sediment and can improve scratch and burnish resistance. Untreated grades are more prone to hard caking in thin waterborne bases.
  • Humidity tolerance. A grade that keeps the film clear under high humidity reduces whitening complaints. Test it, rather than relying on datasheet claims.
  • Dispersibility. Many Indonesian coating plants use high-speed dispersers rather than bead mills. Grades that reach target fineness in a short high-speed step save time and energy.
  • Consistency and documents. Gloss at a fixed dosage should repeat from lot to lot. The SDS should state amorphous silica, and a COA should come with each lot.

A conversion and sourcing checklist

Whether you are converting a solvent-borne matte lacquer, adding a second source or cutting landed cost, a structured trial protects the finish your customers have already approved.

  1. Fix the target. Record the approved panel's 60° and 85° gloss, clarity over dark wood, feel and film build, and keep it as the reference.
  2. Screen the documents. Request SDS, TDS and a sample COA. Check that the COA covers particle size, loss on drying, pH and a structural value such as pore volume or oil absorption.
  3. Build a dosage ladder. Test each candidate at three or four dosages with identical dispersion time, film build and drying, and plot gloss against dosage.
  4. Test in local conditions. Spray and dry panels at the humidity and temperature of your customers' finishing lines, not only in an air-conditioned lab.
  5. Check storage. Heat-age sealed samples, check settling and re-stir, and re-measure viscosity and gloss.
  6. Compare two lots. Confirm lot-to-lot gloss before moving significant volume.
  7. Run a line trial. Finish real furniture on the customer's line, and keep the incumbent as a dual source until results are confirmed.
  8. Review supply terms. Minimum order quantity, lead time, packing and technical support response all affect the real cost of a source.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why are Indonesia wood coatings moving to waterborne?

Export buyers increasingly ask for low-VOC finishes and substance declarations, supply-chain documentation expectations are rising, and waterborne systems reduce solvent exposure and fire risk in spraying workshops. Natural low-sheen design trends add demand for well-formulated waterborne matte finishes.

2. What is the biggest problem with waterborne matte finishes in Indonesia's climate?

High humidity. It slows water release and coalescence, which can cause whitening or blushing in matte clear coats, and it lengthens drying in ambient workshops. Test panels under the humidity your customers' lines actually experience.

3. Which matting agent properties matter for clear finishes on teak?

A fine, tightly distributed particle size for clarity and smooth touch, controlled top size to avoid seeds, a surface treatment that keeps sediment soft, good humidity tolerance and consistent gloss from lot to lot.

4. How much silica matting agent does a waterborne wood topcoat need?

It depends on the binder, film build and gloss target. Censilcoat grades are typically used at 1–4% on total formulation; run a dosage ladder in your own base to find the level that hits your 60° gloss target.

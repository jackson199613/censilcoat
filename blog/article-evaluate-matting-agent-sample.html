How to Evaluate a Matting Agent Sample in 5 Lab Tests

Published: 2026-11-22 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Testing & QC

A free sample bag is the cheapest part of qualifying a new matting agent. The expensive part is a weak evaluation that passes a grade in the lab and then fails on the production line or in the warehouse. To evaluate a matting agent sample properly, compare it side by side with your current grade, in your own formulation, across five lab tests: dispersion and fineness, gloss curve and matting efficiency, clarity, storage stability, and dry-film performance. Each test answers a different question, and together they cover almost every failure that shows up after a switch.

This guide sets out how to prepare the trial, what to measure in each test, which standard methods are commonly used, and how to turn the results into a clear go / no-go decision for R&D and procurement.

Before you evaluate a matting agent sample: set up a fair comparison

Most misleading sample results come from the trial design, not the sample. A few rules keep the comparison fair:

  • Always run a control. Make the reference batch with your current matting agent on the same day, with the same resin lot, the same equipment and the same operator. Never compare a new sample with old data from a datasheet or an archived panel.
  • Use your real formulation. A sample that works in a supplier's starting formula may behave differently in your binder, thickener package and solids level. Test in the product you actually intend to sell.
  • Swap at equal weight first. Start with a 1:1 replacement at the same dosage as the incumbent. Only after you see the result should you fine-tune the dosage.
  • Fix the process. Same order of addition, disperser speed, blade size and time. If the incumbent needs a different process, record it, because that difference is part of the result.
  • Check the paperwork. Compare the sample's TDS and certificate of analysis with the incumbent's: particle size (d50), pore volume or oil absorption, surface area, moisture, pH and surface treatment. Our guide on reading a matting agent COA explains what each line means. Large differences on paper tell you which tests to watch most closely.

Label every jar and panel with the sample code, dosage and date, and keep a simple results sheet with one column per batch. That sheet becomes the qualification record procurement needs later.

Test 1: Dispersion, fineness and viscosity

The first question is whether the sample incorporates and disperses as easily as your current grade with the equipment you have. Precipitated silica matting agents are usually designed to be stirred in rather than milled, and over-grinding can break particles and reduce matting efficiency.

  1. Incorporation. Add the matting agent under moderate shear and note dusting, floating powder, foam and time to wet out.
  2. Fineness of grind. After the set dispersion time, draw down on a Hegman gauge (methods such as ASTM D1210 and ISO 1524 describe the procedure). For matting agents the reading reflects the particle size itself, so compare it with the control rather than chasing a high Hegman number. Look specifically for scattered specks or streaks, which point to agglomerates.
  3. Viscosity. Measure viscosity of both batches at the same temperature, ideally at a low and a high shear rate (for example a rotational viscometer plus a cone-and-plate or flow-cup reading). Porous silica absorbs liquid and can thicken a waterborne system; a noticeably larger increase than the control may force you to rebalance thickeners.
  4. Foam. Check density or visual foam after dispersion. Some matting agents carry air into low-viscosity systems, which later shows up as pinholes.

Pass signal: the sample reaches a comparable fineness reading in the same time, has no seeds, and shifts viscosity by a similar amount to the control. Our article on dispersing silica matting agents without bead milling covers process settings if the first result is borderline.

Test 2: Gloss curve and matting efficiency

Matching gloss at a single dosage is not enough. A gloss curve shows how sensitive the formulation will be to small weighing or film-build variations in production.

  1. Dosage ladder. Prepare at least three to four dosage levels for both sample and control, for example 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% on total formulation, keeping everything else constant.
  2. Controlled application. Apply with a bar or film applicator at a fixed wet film thickness on sealed charts or glass, and on the real substrate if possible. Record dry film thickness, since gloss in matte coatings changes with film build.
  3. Measure gloss. After a defined cure, read 60° and 85° gloss at several points per panel (ASTM D523 and ISO 2813 are the usual references). The 85° geometry separates low-gloss samples better; 60° is the most common specification value. See how to measure gloss correctly on matte panels for panel preparation and geometry choice.
  4. Plot both curves. Gloss versus dosage for sample and control on one chart.

Pass signal: the sample's curve overlaps the control, or reaches your target gloss within a small dosage correction (a few tenths of a percent). A curve that is much steeper means the product will be harder to hold on spec. If a grade needs far more than the incumbent to reach target, it is likely the wrong grade for this system rather than a dosage problem.

Test 3: Clarity, haze and appearance

Two matting agents can give identical gloss and look very different. Clear coats over dark wood, black plastics and deep colours expose differences in particle size distribution and refractive-index match very quickly.

  • Black-panel or dark-substrate drawdown. Apply sample and control side by side on a black chart, black glass or a dark wood panel. View under daylight and at a low angle. Look for a grey, milky or bluish cast.
  • Haze measurement. If a haze meter or a colorimeter is available, measure lightness (L*) over black, or haze on film cast on glass, and compare with the control. Lower lightness over black usually means less whitening from the matting agent.
  • Wood-grain definition. On real timber, check how clearly the grain and pores read through the film. A good clear matte should look deep, not foggy.
  • Surface uniformity. Look for sheen patches, orange peel, cratering and specks, especially on sprayed panels.

Pass signal: on the dark panel, a trained observer cannot reliably pick the sample from the control, or the sample is clearer. Clarity is often the deciding test for premium furniture clears, so include at least one sprayed panel at production film build, not only drawdowns.

Test 4: Storage stability and gloss drift

Many matting problems appear only after the paint has sat in a can. Settling, hard sediment, viscosity rise and gloss drift are the classic post-launch complaints, so an accelerated storage test is essential.

  1. Heat storage. Fill sealed containers of sample and control batches and store them at an elevated temperature; 7 days at 50 °C is a widely used screening condition in the industry. Keep a second set at room temperature for longer-term comparison.
  2. Settling. After storage, inspect the bottom of the container with a spatula and rate how easily any sediment stirs back in. ASTM D869 gives a rating scale for degree of settling. A soft sediment that reincorporates by hand is acceptable for most products; a hard cake is not.
  3. Viscosity and pH. Re-measure and compare with the initial values.
  4. Re-apply and measure gloss. Stir, apply exactly as before, and compare gloss with the fresh panels. A rise of several gloss units means the matting effect is changing during storage.

Pass signal: sediment, viscosity change and gloss change are no worse than the control. For low-viscosity waterborne clears, also check for syneresis (a clear layer on top), which can indicate an interaction between the silica and the thickener.

Test 5: Dry-film performance and feel

The last test checks that the new grade does not weaken the properties your customer actually notices in use. Choose the checks that match the end use:

PropertySimple lab checkWhat to look for vs control
Touch and smoothnessBlind hand-feel ranking by several peopleSame or smoother feel; no grittiness
Burnish and scratchGloss before and after a controlled rub; fingernail and coin scratchSimilar gloss rise; no more visible marks
Water and household chemicalsSpot tests with water, coffee, alcohol or cleaner under a cover glassNo extra whitening or softening
Humidity whiteningApply and dry at high relative humidity, or expose cured film to humidityNo extra milky cast on dark substrate
Adhesion and recoatCross-cut adhesion, including intercoat over a sanded first coatSame rating as control
Hardness and blockingPencil or pendulum hardness; face-to-face block testNo reduction in hardness; no sticking

You do not need every test for every product. A kitchen-cabinet clear needs chemical resistance and burnish data; a plastic housing coating needs adhesion, scratch and touch; a floor finish needs abrasion and burnish resistance. Agree the shortlist with the product owner before the trial starts.

Scoring the results and deciding

Put the five tests on one sheet with three possible outcomes per test: equivalent or better, acceptable with adjustment, or fail. A practical decision rule:

  • All equivalent: approve for a production-scale trial at the same dosage.
  • One or two need adjustment (for example a 0.2–0.3% dosage change or a small thickener correction): fine-tune, repeat the storage test, then move to the line trial.
  • Any fail on clarity, settling or a critical film property: stop and ask the supplier for a different grade rather than reformulating around the problem.

Finish with one production or pilot batch, measured with the same gloss, fineness and viscosity checks used for routine QC. Many plants keep the incumbent grade approved in parallel for the first few deliveries, which lowers the risk of the switch while lot-to-lot consistency of the new supplier is confirmed.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How long does it take to evaluate a matting agent sample?

A first bench comparison of dispersion, gloss curve and clarity can usually be done within a few days. The accelerated storage test adds about a week (for example 7 days at 50 °C), and a production or pilot trial follows after that, so plan on roughly two weeks before a decision.

2. How much sample do I need for a full lab evaluation?

For bench trials, a few hundred grams is enough for dosage ladders, drawdowns and storage jars. A larger sample, such as 2 kg, also allows sprayed panels at production film build and a small pilot batch, which gives more reliable results.

3. Should I test at the same dosage as my current matting agent?

Yes. Start with a 1:1 swap at equal weight so you can see the true difference between the grades. Fine-tune dosage only after the first comparison, and keep any correction small, typically a few tenths of a percent.

4. Which test most often rejects a new matting agent?

It depends on the product. In clear coats over dark wood, clarity and haze are the most common reason to reject a grade. In low-viscosity waterborne systems, settling and gloss drift after heat storage are frequent failure points.

5. Is matching the datasheet values enough to approve a replacement?

No. Similar particle size, pore volume and surface area make a good match likely, but surface treatment, particle size distribution and interaction with your binder and thickener still affect gloss, clarity and stability. Always confirm in your own formulation.

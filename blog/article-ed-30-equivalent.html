ED 30 Equivalent for High-Clarity Clear Coats

Published: 2026-11-23 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Sourcing

When a buyer searches for an ED 30 equivalent, the real requirement is rarely "the same powder." It is a matting agent that delivers the same gloss level, the same water-white clarity over dark wood and the same can stability in an existing clear coat, without a reformulation project. The practical answer is to match the properties that drive appearance (median particle size, particle size distribution, pore volume and surface treatment), then confirm the swap in your own formulation with a short, structured lab trial.

This guide explains which properties actually define a high-clarity matting grade, how to read the published data for ED 30 and a candidate equivalent, the tests that prove clarity, and the mistakes that most often make a "matched" grade look hazy on the panel.

What an ED 30 equivalent needs to match

ED 30 is widely used as a reference grade for clear wood finishes where the film must stay transparent while gloss is reduced. Its supplier's published technical information lists an average particle size of about 5.5 μm and an organic surface treatment. For a substitute to behave the same way in a clear coat, four properties matter more than anything else:

  • Median particle size (d50). d50 sets the size of the micro-roughness the silica creates at the film surface, and therefore how quickly gloss falls with dosage and how fine the surface looks. For high-clarity clears, a d50 in the 5–6 μm band is typical.
  • Width of the size distribution. Two grades with the same d50 can look very different. A broad distribution carries a coarse tail of particles that protrude through thin films and scatter light, which is seen as grey haze over dark substrates. It also carries fines that add viscosity without adding much matting.
  • Pore volume and surface area. Internal porosity lowers the effective density of each particle, so more particles are delivered per gram and matting efficiency rises. Pore volume and surface area also influence how much binder and water the silica takes up, which affects viscosity.
  • Surface treatment. An organic or wax treatment improves suspension and resistance to hard settling, can improve the scratch and hand-feel of the dry film, and reduces the tendency of the silica to whiten in humid conditions.

Oil absorption, pH, moisture content and SiO₂ purity are worth checking on the COA, but they rarely decide whether a substitute passes a clarity test. The size distribution and the surface treatment usually do.

The first screening step is a paper comparison. It is quick, but only useful if you compare like with like. The table below sets out what to look for and why each line matters for a clear-coat application.

Two cautions apply. First, particle size values from different suppliers are often measured with different instruments or dispersion conditions, so a 0.3 μm difference on paper may not be real. Second, a missing value is not a matching value: many datasheets do not publish pore volume or surface area at all. If a figure is not stated, request it or measure it rather than assume it. Waterborne vs solvent-borne: check where the reference grade was designed to work

Many ED-series grades were developed with solvent-borne wood coatings in mind, and the supplier's own literature points waterborne users towards other product lines. In practice, many formulators in Southeast Asia and elsewhere use ED 30 in waterborne acrylic and polyurethane dispersion clears anyway, because it is the grade their technical team knows.

That matters for substitution. In a waterborne system, the matting agent sees a lower-viscosity medium, a higher pH, water as the main volatile and a film formed by particle coalescence rather than solvent evaporation. Silica that is stable in a solvent-borne lacquer can settle harder, build viscosity differently or whiten at high humidity in a waterborne one. A substitute designed and tested for waterborne clears can therefore outperform the reference grade on stability, even when particle size is the same. Define in advance which system you are matching, and run the trial in that system only.

How to prove clarity: the lab tests that matter

A matched datasheet makes a 1:1 replacement likely, not certain. The decision should come from a side-by-side trial in your production formulation, with the incumbent grade as the control. For a high-clarity clear coat, focus on these tests:

  1. Dispersion and fineness. Disperse both grades at the same dosage, speed and time. Check fineness of grind with a Hegman gauge (for example ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210 methods) and record viscosity before and after.
  2. Gloss curve. Make a dosage ladder (for example 1%, 2%, 3% and 4%) for both grades and measure gloss at 60° and 85° (ISO 2813 / ASTM D523). Comparing curves, not a single point, shows whether the substitute is more or less efficient and what dosage correction is needed.
  3. Black-panel clarity. Apply both coatings at production film build over a black panel or dark stained wood, side by side on the same board. Inspect under diffuse light and at a low angle. Any milky or grey cast is the first sign of a coarse tail or poor dispersion. If you have a haze meter, measure on clear film over glass or film substrate as well.
  4. Humidity whitening. Cure panels and condition at high relative humidity (for example 80–85% RH) to check for blushing. This is often where waterborne clears differ most between grades.
  5. Storage stability. Store both batches at elevated temperature (7 days at 50 °C is a common accelerated check), then examine sediment, re-stir behaviour, viscosity and gloss drift.

A practical pass rule for a clear-coat equivalent is: gloss within the specification tolerance at the same dosage or after a small adjustment, no visible difference on the black panel, no blushing worse than the control, and soft, stir-back sediment at most after storage. Common reasons a "matched" grade looks hazy

When a substitute with the right d50 still produces haze, the cause is usually one of the following, and most can be fixed without changing the grade:

  • Coarse tail in the distribution. A grade can hit the d50 target but carry more particles above 10 μm. Ask for d90 and compare fineness of grind directly.
  • Under-dispersion. Agglomerates that are not broken during high-speed dispersion scatter light like oversized particles. Check tip speed, time and the order of addition.
  • Over-dosing to chase gloss. If the substitute is slightly less efficient and the operator adds more, clarity falls. Correct dosage in small steps (a few tenths of a percent) and confirm the gloss target is measured at the right film thickness.
  • Film build differences. Gloss and clarity both shift with dry film thickness. Compare panels made at the same wet film thickness and drying conditions.
  • Refractive-index and binder effects. Silica's refractive index is close to that of many acrylic and polyurethane binders, which is why it can mat without obvious whitening. Changes in binder, coalescent or additive package alter this balance; keep the formula fixed during the comparison.
  • Humidity during cure. Waterborne films cured at high humidity can whiten regardless of the matting agent. Run both grades in the same conditions so the comparison stays fair.

A sourcing checklist for R&D and procurement

A technically sound equivalent still needs to fit the supply side. Before approving a substitute, procurement and R&D should agree on:

  • Specification and COA: which parameters appear on every batch COA (at minimum d50, moisture and pH), and the accepted ranges.
  • Batch consistency: results from at least two or three production lots, or the supplier's batch tolerance on d50.
  • Documentation: current TDS and SDS, and any regulatory statements your customers require for export markets.
  • Commercial terms: minimum order quantity, lead time to your port, packaging format and storage conditions.
  • Change control: how the supplier notifies changes in raw materials, process or plant.
  • Dual sourcing: whether the substitute will replace the reference grade fully or run as a qualified second source.

Approving a second source in this way, even if the incumbent stays, gives a plant a fallback when import lead times stretch or freight costs jump.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Is there a true 1:1 ED 30 equivalent?

A grade with matched particle size, distribution, porosity and surface treatment can usually be swapped at the same dosage, but every formulation is different. Treat 1:1 as the starting point of the trial, then confirm gloss, clarity and stability in your own system and fine-tune dosage by a few tenths of a percent if needed.

2. Which property matters most for clarity in a clear coat?

The width of the particle size distribution, especially the coarse tail. Particles much larger than the d50 protrude through the film and scatter light, which shows up as grey haze over dark wood. Dispersion quality is the second key factor.

3. Can I use an ED 30 equivalent in waterborne coatings?

Yes, but test it in the waterborne system you will actually produce. Settling, viscosity build and humidity whitening can differ between solvent-borne and waterborne systems, so a grade designed for waterborne clears is often the safer choice.

4. How long does it take to qualify a replacement for ED 30?

A bench comparison of dispersion, gloss curve and black-panel clarity takes a few days. Adding an accelerated storage test, for example 7 days at 50 °C, and one production trial typically brings the evaluation to one to two weeks.

5. Should I choose Censil660 or Censil1650A to replace ED 30?

Choose Censil660 (d50 5.0–5.3 μm) for water-white clear topcoats where transparency is the priority. Choose Censil1650A (d50 6.2–6.5 μm) when the goal is a lower-gloss, deep-matte surface rather than maximum clarity.

Burnishing: Why Matte Coatings Turn Glossy Under Rubbing

Published: 2026-11-22 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Troubleshooting

A matte coating that looks perfect on the test panel can turn patchy and shiny within weeks on a chair arm, a table edge or a hallway floor. That localised gloss rise is called burnishing. Burnishing in a matte coating happens when rubbing, polishing or repeated contact flattens the micro-roughness that the matting agent created, so the surface reflects light more like a gloss film. It is one of the most common complaints on matte wood furniture, cabinets, floors and plastic housings, and it is largely preventable at the formulation stage.

This guide explains what physically happens during burnishing, how to test for it, which formulation levers make a difference, and how to choose and dose a silica matting agent so that low gloss survives real use.

What burnishing is, and how it differs from scratching

A matte film looks matte because its surface is rough on a scale of roughly one to several micrometres. Silica particles near the surface, and the shrinkage of the film around them during drying, create bumps that scatter reflected light in many directions instead of mirroring it. Gloss meters read this as low specular reflection.

Burnishing removes or flattens that roughness without necessarily cutting through the film. The result is a polished patch with a higher gloss reading, often most visible at low viewing angles. Three types of surface damage are often confused:

DefectWhat happens to the surfaceTypical appearance
Burnishing (polishing)Peaks are flattened, plastically deformed or smeared; the film is not cutShiny patches, gloss rise in high-contact zones
Scratching / marringGrooves or micro-cracks are cut into the filmVisible lines, often whitish on dark colours
Writing effectFine marks left by a fingernail or hard object, often from transferred material or compressed peaksLight or dark lines that may partly wipe off

The distinction matters because the fixes overlap but are not identical. A harder binder helps against burnishing and scratching; a wax-treated silica tends to help burnishing and writing; a coarser silica can help burnishing but may hurt clarity and touch.

Why burnishing happens on a matte coating: four mechanisms

  1. Soft binder around the silica. If the binder is soft or thermoplastic at use temperature, rubbing pushes it over and around the protruding particles, smoothing the surface. Low-Tg acrylic dispersions and slow-curing films are more prone to this, especially in the first days after application.
  2. Particles that are too fine or too few at the surface. Fine silica gives excellent clarity and a smooth feel, but its bumps are small and easier to level. If the matting agent sinks into the film or is under-dosed, the surface relies on only a thin layer of roughness that wears away quickly.
  3. Particle breakage or pull-out. Under heavy rubbing, protruding silica can fracture or be pulled out of a weak binder matrix. The surface then becomes smoother and shinier, and the loose fragments can act as a polishing compound.
  4. Contamination and transfer. Skin oils, cleaning products with polishing additives, and plasticiser or wax transfer from contact materials fill the valleys between peaks. This is not true burnishing of the film, but it produces the same visual result and is often reported as such.

In practice, two or three of these act together. A soft, fine-matted clear coat that is cleaned with a polish-containing spray is close to a worst case.

How to test burnish resistance

Burnish resistance is easy to test in the lab, and testing early saves expensive field complaints. For architectural latex paints there is a published method, ASTM D6736, Standard Test Method for Burnish Resistance of Latex Paints, which rubs the cured film mechanically and compares gloss before and after. Many wood and plastic coating labs use the same principle with their own in-house conditions. A practical routine:

  1. Prepare matched panels. Apply each formulation at the same wet film thickness on the real substrate (or on sealed black glass or charts for clear coats). Record dry film thickness.
  2. Cure consistently. Test after a defined cure, for example 24 hours and 7 days at 23 °C. Many waterborne films burnish much more at 24 hours than at full cure, and both values matter for the customer.
  3. Measure initial gloss. Read 60° and 85° gloss at several points. The 85° geometry is more sensitive to small changes on matte films, so it usually shows burnishing earlier. Our guide on measuring gloss correctly on matte panels covers geometry choice and panel preparation.
  4. Rub in a controlled way. Use a washability or abrasion tester with a defined pad (cotton cloth, felt or a non-woven pad), a fixed load and a set number of cycles. If no machine is available, a weighted block moved a fixed number of strokes is better than hand rubbing.
  5. Measure again and report the change. Report the gloss increase (ΔGU) at 85° and 60°, plus a visual rating viewed at a low angle under a single light source.
  6. Add a field-type check. Rub with a dry palm, a cloth with a household cleaner, and a fingertip with hand cream. These simple tests often reveal contamination-driven gloss rise that the machine test misses.

Set an internal pass limit based on the end use; a floor finish or a table top needs a much tighter limit than a decorative panel that is rarely touched.

Formulation levers that reduce burnishing

Binder hardness and crosslinking

The binder decides how easily the surface peaks deform. Harder acrylic dispersions, acrylic-polyurethane hybrids, polyurethane dispersions and 2K waterborne systems generally resist burnishing better than soft, single-component acrylics. Crosslinkers and adequate cure (time, temperature, airflow) raise surface hardness. Check that the coalescent level is not so high that the film stays soft for days.

Matting agent particle size and dosage

Coarser matting agents create larger, more robust surface texture, so the film keeps its roughness longer under rubbing. The trade-off is a rougher feel and, in clear coats, more risk of haze. Dosage also matters: a film that only just reaches its gloss target has little reserve. A slightly higher silica level, balanced with a little less of another flattening contribution, often gives a more stable matte.

Surface treatment of the silica

Wax-treated silica matting agents carry a thin organic layer on the particle surface. In general industry experience this wax helps the surface glide under rubbing, which reduces friction-driven polishing and fingernail marks, and it also improves suspension behaviour. Untreated silica can give higher clarity in some systems but usually offers less slip.

Slip and wax additives

Polyethylene or other wax dispersions and silicone slip additives lower the coefficient of friction of the surface. Used at sensible levels they reduce burnishing and marking; overdosed, they can cause recoat adhesion problems, cratering or a greasy feel. Add them in small steps and always test intercoat adhesion.

Film build and application

Very thin films may not hold enough silica at the surface, while very thick films can let particles sink and leave a binder-rich top layer. Keep dry film thickness inside the range used during development. The gloss drift after storage article explains how changes in the paint over time can also shift the starting gloss and make burnishing look worse.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What causes burnishing on a matte coating?

Burnishing happens when rubbing or polishing flattens the micro-roughness created by the matting agent, so the surface reflects light more like a gloss film. Soft binders, fine or under-dosed silica, incomplete cure and polish-containing cleaners are the most common causes.

2. Is burnishing the same as scratching?

No. Burnishing smooths and polishes the surface without cutting it, which raises gloss. Scratching or marring cuts grooves into the film. The two often occur together, and a harder, well-cured binder helps with both.

3. How do I test burnish resistance in the lab?

Measure 60° and 85° gloss on cured panels, rub them with a defined pad, load and number of cycles on a washability or abrasion tester, then measure gloss again. ASTM D6736 describes this principle for latex paints. Report the gloss increase and a visual rating.

4. Does a coarser matting agent always reduce burnishing?

Often it helps, because larger particles create more robust surface texture. But coarser silica can roughen the feel and add haze in clear coats, so balance particle size against clarity and touch, and combine it with a hard, fully cured binder.

5. Do wax additives help against burnishing?

Generally yes. Wax-treated silica and small amounts of wax or slip additives lower surface friction, which reduces polishing and fingernail marks. Overdosing can hurt recoat adhesion or cause cratering, so add in small steps and test intercoat adhesion.

Waterborne Matting Agents: A Formulator's Complete Guide

Published: 2026-11-16 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Core product

A waterborne matting agent is a solid additive, most often amorphous precipitated silica, that lowers the gloss of a water-based coating by creating microscopic roughness at the surface of the dried film. Choosing one is not just a matter of picking the finest powder on the shelf. In water, the matting agent has to wet out in a low-viscosity aqueous phase, stay suspended in the can, survive the pH and surfactant package of the binder, and still end up near the film surface when the water leaves. This guide explains how waterborne matting works, which parameters on a datasheet actually predict performance, typical formulation ranges, how to test a candidate grade, and how to diagnose the most common problems.

It is written for formulators building or reformulating water-based wood, plastic and industrial clear coats, and for purchasing teams who need to understand why two powders with similar particle size can give very different finishes.

How a waterborne matting agent lowers gloss

Gloss is specular reflection: the share of incoming light that leaves the surface at the mirror angle. A perfectly smooth film reflects strongly at that angle and looks glossy. A matting agent works by breaking up that smoothness on a scale of roughly one to several micrometres, so that more light is scattered in other directions and the film reads as satin, matte or dead-matte.

The roughness forms during drying. As water and co-solvent evaporate, the wet film shrinks in thickness and the binder particles pack and coalesce. Rigid matting particles do not shrink, so the binder layer draws down around them and their tops create small bumps on the surface. The more particles that sit close to the surface, and the larger they are relative to the dry film, the lower the gloss.

Waterborne systems add a complication compared with solvent-borne coatings. The volume solids of a water-based clear are often moderate, so the film still shrinks considerably, but the way it shrinks depends on the polymer dispersion, coalescent level, and drying conditions. Fast drying in a tunnel, high humidity or a thick wet film can all change how many particles end up in the surface zone, which is why gloss in waterborne mattes is sensitive to the process and not only the recipe.

The parameters that predict performance

A datasheet for a silica waterborne matting agent usually lists six to ten properties. These are the ones that matter most to the finished film, and what each tells you.

  • Particle size (d50) and distribution. The median size sets the balance between matting power and smoothness. Larger d50 gives stronger gloss reduction per percent added but a rougher feel and more risk of visible texture on thin films. Finer d50 gives smoother touch and better clarity but needs more material for the same gloss. The top end of the distribution (often reported as d90 or d97) controls specks and Hegman readings.
  • Pore volume. Highly porous particles have a low apparent density, so each gram contains more particles and delivers more matting. Pore volume is one of the main reasons two grades with the same d50 mat differently.
  • Surface area (BET). Relates to the internal pore structure and to how strongly the silica interacts with water, surfactants and thickeners. It influences viscosity build and stability.
  • Surface treatment. Wax or organic treatment makes particles easier to wet into the binder, reduces hard sediment, and can improve clarity and scratch resistance. Untreated silica is more hydrophilic and tends to thicken water-based systems more.
  • Oil absorption. A practical proxy for porosity and binder demand. High values mean the silica will tie up more liquid, which affects viscosity and film properties at higher loadings.
  • pH of suspension and moisture. Useful for compatibility checks and lot-to-lot consistency rather than performance on their own.

When comparing grades, look at the combination rather than any single number. Two powders with an identical d50 but different pore volume and treatment can give a several-unit difference in 60° gloss at the same dosage, and noticeably different viscosity and storage behaviour.

Formulating with a waterborne matting agent

There is no universal recipe, but most formulators follow a similar pattern when adding silica to a water-based clear coat.

Typical dosage

Silica matting agents in waterborne clears are commonly used in the low single-digit percent range on total formulation. Satin finishes may need only a small addition, while matte and dead-matte targets need more, and deep matte on thin films may need a coarser grade rather than simply more powder. Always build a dosage ladder (for example three to five levels) in your own base, because the curve of gloss versus dosage is specific to the binder, film thickness and drying conditions.

Order of addition

  1. Charge water, co-solvent or coalescent and the wetting and defoaming additives the formula calls for.
  2. Add the matting agent slowly under moderate stirring to avoid dust and lumps.
  3. Increase to high-speed dispersion and run until the powder is fully wetted and the fineness target is reached.
  4. Let down with the binder dispersion under lower shear, then add thickener, wax and remaining additives.

Some formulators pre-disperse the silica into part of the binder or prepare a silica slurry. Both work; the key is consistent shear and time from batch to batch. Our guide on dispersing silica matting agents without bead milling covers disperser settings in detail.

Interactions to watch

  • Thickeners. Silica can interact with associative and cellulosic thickeners. Add the thickener after the matting agent and check viscosity again after 24 hours.
  • Foam. Powder addition and high shear entrain air. Microfoam in a matte film can look like haze or pinholes, so choose a defoamer that is effective without causing craters.
  • Coalescent level. Changing coalescent alters film formation speed and can shift gloss. Fix the coalescent before tuning matting agent dosage.
  • Wax additives. Wax adds slip and burnish resistance and contributes a little matting, so adjust silica dosage after the wax level is set.

A meaningful evaluation compares the candidate side by side with a reference in the same base, with identical dispersion and film build. A practical test set:

Draw down or spray at the dry film thickness used in production, and dry under conditions close to the real line. A lab panel dried overnight at room temperature can read quite differently from one that passed through a heated tunnel in a few minutes.

Common problems and their likely causes

  • Haze or whitening in clear coats. Often caused by too much or too coarse a matting agent for the film, incomplete coalescence at high humidity, or microfoam. See our article on haze in matte clear coats.
  • Hard sediment in the can. Low-viscosity water-based systems give dense particles time to settle. Wax-treated silica and an appropriate rheology package usually produce a soft, stir-back sediment.
  • Gloss varying between batches or shifts. Check dispersion time and tip speed, film thickness, and drying temperature and airflow before blaming the raw material.
  • Viscosity spikes. High dosage of an untreated or very high-surface-area silica can over-thicken. Consider a treated grade or a slightly coarser grade at lower dosage.
  • Specks and rough feel. Usually incomplete dispersion, agglomerates from damp storage, or a grade too coarse for the film thickness.
  • Burnishing. Matte films that turn glossy when rubbed often benefit from a wax additive or a harder binder, alongside the matting agent.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a waterborne matting agent?

It is a solid additive, most often amorphous precipitated silica, that reduces the gloss of a water-based coating by creating micro-roughness at the surface of the dried film. Wax additives and organic beads are also used, often in combination with silica.

2. How much matting agent should I add to a water-based clear coat?

Silica matting agents are commonly used in the low single-digit percent range on total formulation. The exact level depends on the binder, film thickness, drying conditions and gloss target, so build a dosage ladder in your own base. Censilcoat grades are typically used at 1–4%.

3. Do waterborne matting agents need bead milling?

Many modern silica matting agents, including Censilcoat grades, are designed to disperse with high-speed stirring, typically 15–20 minutes, without bead milling. Check fineness with a Hegman gauge to confirm dispersion.

4. Why does my waterborne matte coating settle hard in the can?

Low-viscosity water-based systems let dense particles settle over time. Untreated silica can form a hard cake. Wax-treated silica combined with a suitable rheology package usually gives a soft sediment that stirs back easily.

5. Why does gloss change between batches of the same waterborne matte formula?

Common causes are differences in dispersion time or tip speed, dry film thickness, drying temperature and airflow, and coalescent level. Control these process variables before attributing variation to the matting agent.

Matting Water-Based PU Wood Lacquers: A Practical Guide

Published: 2026-11-24 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Wood coatings

Water-based PU wood lacquer matting works on the same principle as any matte coating: silica particles roughen the dried film surface on a micro scale so that light is scattered instead of reflected. What makes polyurethane lacquers different is the binder. Polyurethane dispersions (PUDs), PU-acrylic hybrids and two-component waterborne PU systems form harder, more chemically resistant films than plain acrylic emulsions, and they shrink, coalesce and crosslink in ways that change how a matting agent behaves. This guide covers how matting works in each PU system, how to choose and dose the silica, how to incorporate it without seeds or foam, and which tests catch problems before a lacquer reaches a furniture line.

How water-based PU wood lacquer matting works

A waterborne lacquer is applied at perhaps 30–40% solids and dries to a film a fraction of its wet thickness. As water and co-solvent leave, the binder particles pack, deform and coalesce, and the film shrinks around the silica. Particles near the surface end up partly protruding, and that micro-roughness is what lowers gloss. Three things follow from this mechanism:

  • Shrinkage helps matting. Lower-solids lacquers shrink more and tend to matt more efficiently at the same silica loading than higher-solids ones. When a PUD is replaced with a higher-solids grade, gloss usually rises.
  • Film thickness matters. The particle size of the silica has to be sensible relative to dry film thickness. Very coarse particles in a thin coat give a rough, dry feel; very fine particles in a thick coat sink below the surface and matt poorly.
  • Drying speed matters. If the surface skins over quickly, particles have less time to migrate and the final gloss shifts. That is why the same lacquer can read differently in a hot forced-drying tunnel than on an air-dry rack.

Polyurethane adds a twist. Many PUDs form hard, tough films that resist marring and chemicals but are less forgiving of protruding coarse particles. The silica needs to be fine enough to give a smooth hand and low haze, yet efficient enough to reach the gloss target without overloading the lacquer.

1K PUD, PU-acrylic hybrids and 2K waterborne PU: what changes

"Water-based PU lacquer" covers several binder families. Matting behaviour differs between them, so it helps to know which one you are working with before choosing a grade.

In 2K systems the matting agent nearly always goes into component A (the polyol dispersion), never the hardener, because silica carries surface silanol groups and some moisture that can react with isocyanate. Gloss should be measured on panels sprayed at the start, middle and end of the stated pot life, since viscosity rise and partial reaction can shift the final sheen. Choosing the matting agent for a PU wood lacquer

Most waterborne PU wood lacquers are matted with amorphous precipitated silica, often surface-treated with wax. When comparing grades, these parameters do most of the work:

  • Particle size (d50 and top size). For furniture topcoats at typical dry film thicknesses, fine grades in roughly the 4–7 μm d50 range are common. Finer particles give better clarity and a smoother touch; slightly coarser ones give more matting per kilogram. Top size controls seeds and grit.
  • Pore volume. High pore volume means more particles per gram, which generally means more matting efficiency at a given dosage and less impact on film properties.
  • Surface treatment. Wax treatment is the norm for low-viscosity waterborne lacquers because it converts hard sediment into soft, stir-back sediment and improves mar and burnish resistance. See our comparison of wax-treated vs untreated silica for when the wax helps and when it may affect recoat adhesion.
  • Refractive index match and haze. Silica's refractive index is close to that of many PU binders, which is why well-dispersed silica gives low haze over dark wood. Poor dispersion, oversized particles or overdosing still produce a grey or milky cast.

A practical rule: start with a fine, universal grade for clear furniture lacquers, move to a high-clarity grade if the lacquer goes over dark walnut or wenge, and move to a coarser, more efficient grade only when you need deep matte below about 10 GU at 60° and can accept a slightly drier feel.

Formulating: dosage, order of addition and dispersion

Dosage

Silica dosage in waterborne PU wood lacquers is usually expressed on total formulation. Start with a ladder, for example 1%, 2%, 3% and 4%, and plot 60° gloss against dosage at a fixed dry film thickness. Typical targets in furniture are semi-gloss (around 30–50 GU), satin or silk matte (around 15–30 GU) and full matte (below about 10 GU). If a grade needs much more than 4–5% to reach the target, it is usually more productive to select a more efficient grade than to keep adding silica, since overdosing raises viscosity and can hurt clarity, flexibility and water resistance.

Order of addition

  1. Charge the PUD (or polyol component) and any water.
  2. Add defoamer and substrate-wetting agent under slow stirring.
  3. Add co-solvent or coalescent slowly, pre-diluted if the dispersion is sensitive to shock.
  4. Add the silica gradually into the vortex, increasing speed as it wets out.
  5. Disperse at high speed for the specified time, then reduce speed.
  6. Add wax emulsion, slip and levelling additives, then thickener last to adjust final viscosity.

Adding thickener before the silica makes wetting harder and can trap air. Adding silica to a very thin base without wetting agent can leave it floating on the surface. Our guide on dispersing silica matting agents without bead milling covers disperser speed and blade sizing in more detail.

Dispersion and foam

Modern matting silicas are supplied at their final particle size, so the disperser only has to wet the powder and break up loose agglomerates; it is not grinding. Over-dispersion can warm the batch and entrain air. Check fineness of grind on a Hegman gauge after dispersion and again after let-down; a reading consistent with the d50 of the grade and free of individual coarse specks is the goal. Foam is a frequent issue in PU lacquers because PUDs contain their own stabilising surfactants or ionic groups. Choose a defoamer compatible with the binder, and check for craters and microbubbles on sprayed panels, not only in the can.

Tests to run before approving a matted PU lacquer

Keep everything except the variable under test fixed: substrate, sanding, wet film thickness, flash-off and drying schedule. A practical set of checks:

  • Gloss at 60° and 85° per ISO 2813 or ASTM D523, at a fixed dry film thickness. The 85° reading separates samples that look identical at 60° in the low-gloss range.
  • Clarity on a black panel and on the darkest stain the customer uses, compared under consistent light.
  • In-can stability: sealed storage at room temperature and accelerated ageing (for example 7 days at 50 °C), then rate sediment, re-measure viscosity and gloss.
  • Burnish and mar: gloss before and after a defined number of rubs with a dry cloth, and a fingernail/coin scratch comparison.
  • Cross-cut adhesion per ISO 2409 or ASTM D3359, including recoat over sanded and unsanded first coats if a matted lacquer will be used as more than one coat.
  • Liquid resistance: furniture specifications often reference EN 12720 for resistance to cold liquids; check water, coffee, alcohol and household cleaner marks on matte panels, since matting can make stains more visible.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How much matting agent does a water-based PU wood lacquer need?

It depends on the gloss target, film thickness and binder solids. Run a dosage ladder (for example 1, 2, 3 and 4% on total formulation) and plot 60° gloss against dosage. If a grade needs well over 4–5% to reach target, choose a more efficient grade rather than overdosing.

2. Where should the silica go in a 2K waterborne PU lacquer?

Into component A, the polyol dispersion. Keep silica out of the isocyanate hardener, and check gloss on panels sprayed at the start, middle and end of the pot life, because gloss can drift as the mixed lacquer ages.

3. Why does my matte PU lacquer look hazy over dark wood?

Common causes are overdosing, a grade with too coarse a top size, incomplete dispersion and incomplete coalescence, especially at high humidity. Check the lacquer on a black panel, reduce dosage or move to a finer, high-clarity grade, and confirm the coalescent level.

4. Do I need a bead mill to disperse matting silica in a PUD?

Usually not. Matting silicas are supplied at their final particle size, so high-speed dispersion to wet the powder and break loose agglomerates is enough. Over-dispersing can warm the batch and entrain air.

5. Which Censilcoat grade should I start with for a water-based PU wood lacquer?

Censil351C (d50 4.8–5.1 μm), a fine universal grade benchmarked against ED 3, is a good starting point for clear furniture and cabinet lacquers. Censil361C suits higher matting efficiency and Censil660 suits water-white clears over dark wood. Dosage is 1–4% on total formulation.

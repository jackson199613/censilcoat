Matting Agents for Plastic Coatings: A Grade Selection Guide

Published: 2026-11-13 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Plastic coatings

Choosing a matting agent for plastic coatings is harder than choosing one for wood. Plastic topcoats are often applied as very thin films, they must stick to low-energy substrates, and the brief usually specifies a feel as well as a gloss number. A grade that works in a 40 μm furniture lacquer can look rough, gritty or patchy at 15 μm on an ABS housing. This guide explains how silica matting works in thin plastic films, which parameters decide the grade, how to match grades to common plastic applications, and how to test a candidate before it reaches the production line.

It is written for formulators of waterborne coatings for consumer electronics, automotive interior trim, appliances and other plastic parts, and for buyers who need to understand why "the same" matting agent from two suppliers can behave differently on a thin film.

Why plastic coatings make matting harder

A silica matting agent lowers gloss by creating fine roughness at the film surface: as the coating dries and shrinks, the non-shrinking porous particles push up micro-bumps that scatter light. On plastic parts, several conditions make that mechanism harder to control:

  • Thin dry films. Many plastic topcoats are applied at roughly 10–25 μm dry film thickness (DFT), and some are thinner still. When the particle size is large relative to the film, individual particles stand proud of the surface and show as texture, seeds or a sandy feel.
  • Appearance and touch are both specified. Electronics and interior-trim customers judge the surface by hand as well as by gloss meter. Smoothness, soft-touch, fingerprint visibility and scratch marks are often part of acceptance.
  • Adhesion comes first. Substrates such as PP, and to a lesser extent ABS and PC, need the right binder, primer or surface treatment. Silica loading raises the pigment volume of a clear film, and excessive loading can weaken cohesion and adhesion.
  • Low gloss targets. Plastic parts are frequently specified in the deep-matte range, often single-digit gloss units at 60°, which pushes formulators toward higher loadings or coarser grades exactly where the film is thinnest.
  • Waterborne film formation. In acrylic and polyurethane dispersions, drying rate, coalescent level and humidity all shift final gloss, and low application viscosity makes settling a constant concern.

The parameters that decide the grade

Most selection mistakes come from looking at one number on a datasheet. Compare these together:

A practical rule used across the industry is to keep the particle size comfortably below the dry film thickness for smooth finishes, and to accept a coarser grade only when a textured or extreme low-gloss surface is wanted and the film is thick enough to hold it. The narrower the distribution, the closer to the film thickness you can work, because oversize particles are what cause visible defects.

Wax treatment is particularly useful on plastic. It tends to give a smoother, silkier feel, improves resistance to scratch and burnishing marks, and produces a soft, stir-back sediment rather than a hard cake in low-viscosity waterborne coatings.

Formulation guidelines for matte plastic topcoats

  1. Fix the film build first. Agree the target DFT with the applicator before choosing a grade. A grade selected at 25 μm may look rough at 12 μm and glossy at 35 μm.
  2. Run a dosage ladder. Prepare the same base at, for example, 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% matting agent on total formulation and plot 60° and 85° gloss against loading. If the curve flattens before the target, switch to a more efficient grade rather than adding more silica.
  3. Watch viscosity on thin systems. Porous silica absorbs binder and water and builds viscosity. Overdosing to chase gloss is a common source of viscosity spikes and specks in thin plastic films; adjust with the rheology package, not by thinning the matting level away.
  4. Protect adhesion. Check cross-hatch adhesion (ISO 2409 or ASTM D3359) at the highest matting level you plan to use, on the real substrate and with its real pretreatment, because high silica loading can reduce adhesion.
  5. Balance coalescent and drying. Too much coalescent or slow, humid drying gives the binder time to flow over particles and raises gloss. Use the minimum needed for good film formation at your lowest line temperature.
  6. Use wax additives deliberately. Wax emulsions can improve scratch and mar resistance and help matting, but check recoatability and adhesion of any subsequent coats.
  7. Add slip and anti-fingerprint performance last. Silicone or wax additives change surface feel and fingerprint visibility; evaluate them after the matting level is settled so you are not chasing two variables at once.

Dispersion: wet, don't grind

Modern matting silicas are supplied already micronised, so the aim in production is to wet the powder and break up soft agglomerates without changing the particle size. Over-milling can reduce particle size and therefore matting efficiency.

  • Charge water, dispersing additive and defoamer, then add the silica gradually under moderate stirring.
  • Disperse on a high-speed disperser at a fixed speed and time, and keep both identical batch to batch; dispersion energy is a frequent cause of gloss variation.
  • Check fineness on a Hegman gauge (ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210). For thin plastic films, no visible coarse particles above your film thickness is the practical requirement.
  • Let down with binder at lower speed and add thickeners last. Filtering through a suitable mesh before spraying reduces seeds on cosmetic parts.

Lab test plan for a candidate grade

Before qualifying a grade, test it in your own base and on your own substrate. A compact plan:

  • Gloss at 60° and 85° (ISO 2813 / ASTM D523) at the target DFT, plus one thinner and one thicker film to see how sensitive the gloss is to film build.
  • Surface feel and appearance: compare by hand and under raking light against the current reference, looking for seeds, texture and patchiness.
  • Adhesion by cross-hatch on each substrate, before and after any humidity or water-immersion test required by the specification.
  • Scratch, mar and burnish: fingernail or stylus scratch, and a rub test with gloss measured before and after.
  • Chemical and stain resistance to agents named by the end customer, such as hand cream, sunscreen or cleaning solutions.
  • Storage stability: oven storage (40–50 °C for several weeks is a common choice), then check sediment type, viscosity and gloss drift.

If you are replacing a grade already in production, keep the loading identical in the first trial and compare side by side; our guide to replacing imported matting agents 1:1 covers a step-by-step qualification route for thin waterborne plastic coatings.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the best matting agent for plastic coatings?

There is no single best grade. Choose by dry film thickness, gloss target and required feel: a medium, narrow-distribution silica for thin matte films, a wax-treated deep-matte grade for premium tactile surfaces, and a coarse grade only for extreme low gloss where texture is acceptable. Confirm with a dosage ladder in your own base.

2. What particle size should a matting agent have for thin plastic films?

Keep the particle size comfortably below the dry film thickness for smooth finishes, and pay attention to the top cut, because oversize particles cause seeds and grit. For films of around 10–15 μm, a medium grade with a narrow distribution is a common starting point.

3. How much matting agent should I add to a plastic topcoat?

It depends on the grade, binder, film build and gloss target. Censilcoat grades are used at 1–4% on total formulation. If you need far more to reach the target, switch to a more efficient grade instead of overdosing, which can cause viscosity spikes, specks and weaker adhesion.

4. Does a matting agent affect adhesion on plastic?

It can. Higher silica loading increases the pigment content of a clear film and can reduce cohesion and adhesion. Run cross-hatch adhesion tests at your highest planned matting level, on the real substrate and pretreatment, including after any humidity test in the specification.

5. Why use a wax-treated silica in plastic coatings?

Wax treatment generally gives a smoother, silkier feel, better scratch and burnish resistance, and a soft, stir-back sediment instead of a hard cake in low-viscosity waterborne coatings. These properties matter on cosmetic plastic parts that are touched and handled.

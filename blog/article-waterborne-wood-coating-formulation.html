Waterborne Wood Coating Formulation: Where the Matting Agent Fits

Published: 2026-11-16 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Wood coatings

A waterborne wood coating formulation is a balance of a polymer dispersion, water, coalescent, additives and, for any finish below full gloss, a matting agent. The matting agent is usually a small share of the recipe, yet it decides how the finish looks and feels, and it interacts with almost every other ingredient. This guide walks through the building blocks of a water-based wood lacquer, shows exactly where the matting agent goes in the order of addition, explains how the binder, wax, thickener and coalescent change its behaviour, and sets out a lab workflow for hitting a gloss target consistently.

It is written for R&D chemists developing or converting water-based wood topcoats and sealers, and for procurement teams who want to understand why swapping one silica for another is never just a price decision.

The building blocks of a waterborne wood coating formulation

Most clear water-based wood lacquers for furniture, cabinets, doors and floors share a similar architecture. Two things stand out. First, the matting agent is one of the few solid particles in a clear formula, so it dominates the optical behaviour of the film. Second, it sits in a water phase full of surfactants and thickeners, so its surface chemistry matters as much as its particle size. Where the matting agent goes in the order of addition

The order of addition is where many gloss problems start. A typical sequence for a waterborne wood coating formulation using a silica that disperses under high-speed stirring looks like this:

  1. Charge the grind water. Add part of the water plus the wetting agent and a portion of the defoamer. If the coalescent is compatible with your binder in this order, some formulators add it here; others add it later, pre-diluted.
  2. Add the matting agent under moderate stirring. Sprinkle the powder in steadily to avoid lumps and dusting. Silica has a low bulk density, so this step takes longer than people expect.
  3. Disperse at high speed. Raise the disperser speed and run for a fixed time until the powder is wetted and the Hegman target is met. Record tip speed, time and temperature.
  4. Let down with the binder. Reduce shear and add the polymer dispersion. Many dispersions are shear-sensitive, so avoid running the high-speed stage after the binder is in unless the supplier allows it.
  5. Add the finishing additives. Coalescent (if not added earlier), wax emulsion, remaining defoamer, substrate wetting agent and finally the thickener to adjust viscosity.
  6. Rest and re-check. Measure viscosity and draw a gloss panel after a defined rest time, often the next day, because foam release and thickener build continue after mixing.

An alternative used in some plants is to disperse the silica directly into part of the binder, or to keep a stock silica slurry. Both can work. What matters is that the matting agent always receives the same shear history, because under- or over-dispersion moves gloss. How the binder changes matting behaviour

The same matting agent at the same dosage can give very different gloss in different binders. The main reasons are film shrinkage, how fast the film forms, and the binder's own surface texture.

  • Acrylic dispersions. Widely used for interior furniture and cabinets. Harder acrylics with higher MFFT need more coalescent, and the drying profile strongly influences how many silica particles end up at the surface.
  • Polyurethane dispersions (PUD) and acrylic-PU hybrids. Often chosen for better scratch, abrasion and chemical resistance, for example in floors and tabletops. Some PUDs form very smooth, level films and can need a little more matting agent for the same gloss.
  • Two-component (2K) waterborne systems. A crosslinker improves resistance, but the mixed coating's properties can change over the pot life. Gloss may drift as the mix ages, so test panels across the pot life, not only right after mixing.
  • Self-crosslinking and UV-curable dispersions. These have their own film-formation behaviour; follow the binder supplier's guidance on solids and film build, then tune matting within that window.

Binder particle size and volume solids also matter. A lower-solids formula shrinks more as water evaporates, which tends to draw the film down around the silica and lower gloss. If you change binder or solids, expect to re-ladder the matting agent rather than carry over the old dosage.

Balancing matting agent, wax, thickener and coalescent

The matting agent does not act alone. Four interactions explain most of the "mystery" gloss shifts in waterborne wood coatings.

Wax

Wax emulsions and micronised waxes add slip and scratch resistance and help prevent burnishing, but they also contribute some matting and can change surface feel. Set the wax level first for the resistance and feel you need, then adjust silica to the final gloss.

Thickener

Silica has a high internal surface and can interact with associative thickeners and surfactants. The result can be viscosity that keeps rising over the first day, or occasionally falls. Add the thickener after the silica is dispersed and always compare viscosity at the same rest time. Good anti-settling rheology also helps keep the silica suspended, which keeps gloss consistent from the top to the bottom of the can.

Coalescent

Coalescent controls how long the film stays open. More coalescent or slower-evaporating co-solvent lets the film level for longer, which can let silica sink slightly from the surface and raise gloss. Too little coalescent risks poor film formation and whitening, particularly at high humidity. Fix the coalescent level before final tuning of the matting agent. Our troubleshooting articles cover the film-formation side of high-humidity whitening in more detail.

Defoamer and wetting agents

Powder addition and high shear trap air. Microfoam left in a matte clear coat looks like haze or a grey cast. On the other hand, an over-aggressive defoamer can cause craters. Choose the defoamer early and keep it constant while you adjust gloss.

Sealer vs topcoat, film build and drying

Most wood finishing systems include a sealer or primer coat under the topcoat. The matting agent normally belongs mainly in the topcoat, because gloss is set by the outermost surface. A sealer may contain a small amount of silica or other fillers for sandability, but heavy matting in the sealer can reduce clarity and depth of grain under the topcoat.

Film build and drying matter as much as dosage:

  • Dry film thickness. At a fixed dosage, a thinner film usually reads lower in gloss because particles are closer to the surface relative to film thickness. Spray panels at production film build, not at a convenient lab drawdown.
  • Drying temperature and airflow. Fast, warm, high-airflow drying generally freezes particles near the surface and lowers gloss; slow, cool drying lets the film level and can raise it. A lab panel dried overnight at room temperature may not match a line with a heated tunnel.
  • Humidity. High humidity slows water loss and can extend open time, affecting both gloss and clarity.
  • Substrate. Open-pore oak behaves differently from closed-grain maple or a filled MDF panel. Test on the wood species and sealer used in production.

A lab workflow for hitting the gloss target

Treat matting as the last tuning step in a waterborne wood coating formulation, once the binder, coalescent, wax and rheology package are fixed. A practical sequence:

  1. Prepare a base without matting agent and confirm film formation, clarity and resistance.
  2. Choose a grade by target: a finer grade for high clarity and smooth touch, a coarser or more efficient grade for matte and dead-matte.
  3. Build a dosage ladder of three to five levels with identical dispersion time and tip speed.
  4. Apply at production film build and dry under production-like conditions.
  5. Measure, then plot gloss against dosage and pick the level that sits on a flat part of the curve, where small weighing errors move gloss least.
  6. Run heat-age storage and re-measure gloss and viscosity before signing off.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. At what stage should the matting agent be added to a waterborne wood coating?

Usually in the grind stage: add the silica to water with wetting agent and defoamer, disperse at high speed to the fineness target, then let down with the binder under lower shear. Avoid high shear after shear-sensitive dispersions are added unless the binder supplier allows it.

2. How much matting agent does a waterborne wood lacquer need?

Silica matting agents are commonly used in the low single-digit percent range on total formulation. The right level depends on binder, film thickness, drying and gloss target, so build a dosage ladder in your own base. Censilcoat grades are typically used at 1–4%.

3. Should the sealer contain matting agent as well as the topcoat?

Gloss is set mainly by the topcoat, so most of the matting agent belongs there. Heavy matting in the sealer can reduce clarity and depth of grain; some sealers carry a small amount of silica or filler for sandability.

4. Why did gloss change when I switched binder?

Binders differ in film shrinkage, solids, levelling and film-formation speed, all of which change how many silica particles sit at the surface. Re-ladder the matting agent after any binder or solids change rather than carrying over the old dosage.

5. Does wax replace the silica matting agent?

Generally no. Wax adds slip, scratch and burnish resistance and contributes some gloss reduction, but silica does most of the matting in clear wood coatings. Set the wax level first, then tune silica to the final gloss.

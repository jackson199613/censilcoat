How to Disperse Silica Matting Agents Without Bead Milling

Published: 2026-11-13 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Formulation

Good matting agent dispersion does not need a bead mill. Modern silica matting agents are supplied already micronised to their working particle size, so the job in the plant is to wet the powder and break up the loose agglomerates that form in the bag, not to grind anything smaller. A correctly set high-speed disperser does that in minutes. Milling, on the other hand, can reduce particle size, cut matting efficiency and add heat and cost. This guide explains why matting silica behaves differently from pigments, how to set up a disperser, the order of addition that works in waterborne systems, how to check the result, and how to fix the faults that usually get blamed on "poor dispersion".

It is written for formulators and production chemists making waterborne wood and plastic coatings, and for buyers who want to know what a supplier means when a datasheet says "no bead milling required".

Why matting silica does not need grinding

Pigments such as iron oxides or organic colours are made of very fine primary particles that cluster into hard aggregates; developing their colour strength takes high shear, often in a bead mill. Precipitated silica matting agents are different. The particle you buy is itself the functional unit: a porous, micron-sized particle whose size and pore volume were set during manufacture and milling at the silica plant. In the can, it only needs to be:

  • Wetted, so air in and around the particles is replaced by liquid;
  • Deagglomerated, so the soft clusters formed during packing, transport and storage separate into individual particles;
  • Stabilised, so the particles stay separated and suspended until application.

Matting comes from those particles creating micro-roughness at the film surface as the coating dries and shrinks. If you mill them, two things tend to happen. Particle size falls, so each gram produces less surface roughness and you need more silica for the same gloss. And with surface-treated grades, aggressive grinding can damage the wax or organic treatment that controls hand-feel, scratch resistance and sediment behaviour. That is why the general industry practice for matting silica is high-speed dispersion rather than milling, and why over-dispersion is a recognised cause of gloss variation.

Setting up the high-speed disperser

A saw-tooth (Cowles-type) disperser blade works by creating a strong shear zone at the blade edge and a rolling, "doughnut" flow pattern that pulls powder from the surface down into that zone. Getting the geometry right matters more than running at maximum speed. Common rules of thumb from dispersion practice:

SettingTypical guidelineWhy it matters
Blade diameter (D) vs vessel diameterVessel roughly 2–3 × DToo large a vessel leaves dead zones where powder floats or settles

Tip speed, not rpm

Because laboratory and production dispersers have different blade sizes, specify tip speed rather than rpm. Tip speed (m/s) = π × blade diameter (m) × rpm ÷ 60. A 50 mm lab blade at 3,000 rpm and a 400 mm production blade at about 375 rpm both run at roughly 7.9 m/s. Pigment grinds on dispersers are commonly run in the high teens to low twenties of metres per second; matting silica usually needs less, because the goal is wetting and deagglomeration rather than size reduction. The right figure for your base is the lowest tip speed that reliably gives a clean fineness reading and stable gloss in the time you allow, and it should be fixed in the batch instructions so the lab and the plant transfer cleanly.

Order of addition for waterborne systems

There are two common routes. Which one you use depends on whether the matting agent goes into a pigmented mill base or is added to a clear system.

Route A: dispersing in the grind stage

  1. Charge water and any co-solvent; start the disperser at low speed.
  2. Add the wetting and dispersing additive and part of the defoamer, so foam is controlled before powder brings in air.
  3. Add the matting agent gradually into the vortex, not all at once, raising speed as the batch thickens to keep the rolling flow.
  4. Once all powder is in, run at the set tip speed for the specified time (for many matting grades, 15–20 minutes is typical).
  5. Check fineness, then let down with binder at reduced speed.

Route B: post-adding to the letdown

  1. Prepare the binder letdown, with coalescent pre-diluted and added slowly to avoid shocking the emulsion or dispersion.
  2. Reduce the batch to moderate speed; add defoamer if needed.
  3. Sift the matting agent in slowly, then disperse for the set time at a tip speed the binder tolerates without shear damage or excessive foam.
  4. Add thickeners last, after the silica is fully wetted, and adjust viscosity.

Route B is common for clear wood and plastic topcoats. Its main risk is binder sensitivity: some acrylic emulsions and polyurethane dispersions do not like prolonged high shear, so check the resin supplier's guidance. An alternative used by many plants is a matting paste: a pre-dispersed concentrate of silica, water, dispersant and a small amount of binder or rheology additive, made in bulk and stirred into several products. It improves consistency and reduces dust handling at the batch tank.

Checking that dispersion is complete

Because matting silica is meant to stay at its supplied particle size, the checks are about confirming that agglomerates are gone and that the batch matches the approved standard, not about reaching a particular "grind".

  • Fineness of grind on a Hegman gauge (ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210). A matted coating will not read as fine as an unmatted one, because the gauge registers the matting particles themselves. Set the acceptance value from a well-dispersed reference batch of the same formula, and look for scattered specks or streaks that indicate undispersed agglomerates.
  • Gloss at 60° and 85° (ISO 2813 / ASTM D523) on a drawdown at the target film thickness, compared with the standard. A batch that reads glossier than standard is often under-dispersed; one that reads glossier after extra dispersion time may be over-dispersed.
  • Viscosity at a fixed time after dispersion. Porous silica keeps absorbing liquid for a while, so measure at the same point every time.
  • Drawdown appearance under raking light, checking for seeds, craters from foam and patchy sheen.

Record tip speed, time and batch temperature on every batch sheet. When a gloss complaint arrives months later, those three numbers are usually the first thing you will want.

A simple lab protocol to set your dispersion window

Before a new matting agent goes into production, establish how sensitive it is to dispersion energy in your own base:

  1. Make one base batch large enough for five samples at the same matting level.
  2. Disperse the samples for different times at a fixed tip speed, for example 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 minutes.
  3. Draw down at the target film thickness and measure 60° and 85° gloss after full cure.
  4. Read fineness and viscosity for each sample.
  5. Choose the time at which gloss and fineness have stabilised, and make sure the slightly longer times do not raise gloss again.

A grade with a wide, flat window is easier to run in production because operator variation has little effect on gloss. A narrow window means the batch instructions must be followed closely. Repeat the test if you change the disperser, the batch size or the binder.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Do silica matting agents need bead milling?

Usually not. Precipitated silica matting agents are supplied already micronised, so they only need to be wetted and deagglomerated. A high-speed disperser does this; bead milling can reduce particle size, lower matting efficiency and damage surface treatments.

2. How long should a matting agent be dispersed?

It depends on the grade, base viscosity and tip speed. For many matting grades 15–20 minutes on a high-speed disperser is typical. Set your own window by dispersing samples for different times and choosing the point where gloss and fineness stop changing.

3. What tip speed should I use for matting agent dispersion?

Use the lowest tip speed that reliably gives a clean fineness reading and stable gloss in your base. Matting silica generally needs less energy than a pigment grind. Specify tip speed (π × blade diameter × rpm ÷ 60) rather than rpm so lab and production settings transfer.

4. Why does my matte coating show a coarse Hegman reading?

A matted coating always reads coarser than an unmatted one, because the gauge registers the matting particles themselves. Compare against a well-dispersed reference batch of the same formula and look for scattered specks or streaks, which indicate undispersed agglomerates.

5. Can over-dispersion change gloss?

Yes. Excessive time or shear can break down particles or damage their surface treatment, so the same loading gives less matting and gloss rises. Fix tip speed and time in the batch instructions and record them on every batch sheet.

Writing Effect and Fingernail Marks on Matte Coatings

Published: 2026-11-26 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Troubleshooting

Run a fingernail across a fresh matte table top and you may see a pale line appear, as if someone had written on the surface with chalk. On a dark cabinet door, a set of keys can leave grey streaks. Formulators call this the writing effect, and on a matte coating it is one of the fastest ways to lose a customer: the finish is not scratched in the usual sense, yet it looks marked after the first day of use. The writing effect comes from the same micro-roughness that makes the film matte, so it cannot be removed by simply adding less matting agent. It has to be managed through silica selection, surface slip, binder hardness and cure.

This guide explains the mechanisms behind fingernail and metal marks, how to test them reproducibly, which formulation levers work, and how to choose a silica grade that keeps the surface clean without sacrificing clarity or touch.

What the writing effect on a matte coating actually is

A matte film scatters light because its surface carries bumps roughly one to several micrometres high, formed by silica particles near the surface and by film shrinkage around them during drying. When a hard object is drawn across that surface, three different things can happen, and each produces a visible "written" line:

  • Material transfer. The silica peaks act like a very fine file. They scrape keratin from a fingernail, or metal from a coin, key or ring, and the textured surface traps the debris. Nails tend to leave light marks; metals often leave grey or dark marks, which is why the metal variant is sometimes called metal marking.
  • Local flattening. Pressure from the object flattens or smears the binder-rich peaks along a narrow track. The line reflects more light than its surroundings, so it looks glossy at low viewing angles. This is effectively a very localised form of burnishing.
  • Micro-damage. In a brittle or under-cured film, the stress concentrated at protruding particles can crack the binder around them or pull particles partly out. The damaged zone scatters light differently and usually appears whitish, especially on dark colours.

In real complaints two of these often occur together. A useful first step is always to establish which one dominates, because the fixes differ.

Why some matte films mark easily and others do not

Particle size and distribution

Coarse particles and the oversized tail of a broad particle size distribution protrude furthest above the binder. They give a strong matting effect but also act as the most aggressive abrasive points against a nail or a coin. A fine matting agent with a narrow distribution produces many small, evenly spaced peaks instead of a few tall ones, which generally lowers the abrasive action and gives a smoother hand-feel. The trade-off is lower matting efficiency per kilogram, so dosage may need to rise slightly to hold the same gloss.

Surface friction and silica treatment

Friction decides whether an object glides across the peaks or grates against them. Untreated silica presents a hard, high-friction inorganic surface. Wax-treated silica carries a thin organic layer that, in general formulating experience, lowers surface friction and reduces both marking and pick-up of debris. Our article on wax-treated versus untreated silica matting agents covers the wider trade-offs of the treatment.

Binder hardness, elasticity and cure

The binder holds the silica in place and determines whether peaks deform. A very soft, thermoplastic film lets peaks smear and produces glossy writing lines; a very hard but brittle film can crack around particles and produce white lines. Crosslinked polyurethane dispersions, acrylic-PU hybrids and 2K waterborne systems usually give a better balance than soft single-component acrylics. Cure matters as much as chemistry: many waterborne films mark far more at 24 hours than after 7 days, so the timing of packing and handling is part of the problem.

Film build and particle anchoring

When the dry film is thin relative to the particle size, a larger part of each particle protrudes and the anchoring is weak. This increases both abrasion of objects and particle pull-out. Keeping the dry film thickness comfortably above the d50 of the matting agent, and consistent with the development panels, reduces the risk.

How to test fingernail and metal marking

Writing resistance is rarely covered by a single standard, so most labs combine a published scratch method with simple in-house tests. For a controlled, instrument-based check, ISO 1518-1, the constant-loading scratch resistance method for paints and varnishes, draws a loaded stylus across the film and is useful for ranking binders and cure conditions. For the writing effect itself, a practical routine is:

  1. Use a contrasting substrate. Apply the coating on a dark or black substrate (or a dark-stained wood panel for clear coats), because light marks are hardest to see on pale colours. Record wet and dry film thickness.
  2. Test at two cure states. For example, after 24 hours and after 7 days at 23 °C. The early value predicts problems in packing and transport; the later value predicts the end user's experience.
  3. Fingernail test. Draw a fingernail across the panel at a steady angle and moderate pressure. Use the same operator for a series, because nail shape and hardness vary from person to person.
  4. Metal marking test. Draw the edge of a coin, a key or a standard metal rod across the surface. Note whether the mark is grey or dark (metal transfer) or glossy (flattening).
  5. Wipe-off check. Wipe each mark once with a soft, damp cloth, or rub gently with a soft eraser. Rate the mark before and after on a simple 1–5 visual scale under low-angle light.
  6. Compare against a reference. Always run the current production formula, or a known good panel, beside each trial. Relative ranking is far more reliable than absolute ratings for this test.

If the mark wipes off, focus on surface friction and particle protrusion. If it remains, focus on binder hardness, cure and film damage.

A sensible order of work is to confirm cure and film build first, because they cost nothing in raw materials, then adjust silica grade and treatment, and only then fine-tune slip additives. Adding slip additive to a film that is simply under-cured hides the symptom for a while but does not fix it.

Quick troubleshooting checklist

  • White lines that wipe off: nail debris trapped in the texture. Try a finer or wax-treated silica and a small slip addition.
  • Grey or dark lines from keys and coins: metal transfer from abrasive peaks. Reduce protruding coarse particles and increase surface slip.
  • Glossy lines that stay: flattened peaks in a soft film. Increase binder hardness or crosslinking and check coalescent level and cure.
  • White lines that stay: micro-cracking or particle pull-out. Check film flexibility, particle wetting and dispersion; avoid over-grinding the silica.
  • Marks only in the first days: incomplete cure. Extend drying or force-dry before stacking and packing.
  • Some batches mark more than others: variable film build or dispersion. Standardise dispersion time, check fineness and gloss on every batch, and control spray settings.

Batch variation in gloss often travels with batch variation in marking, so the controls described in our gloss drift after storage article also help here.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the writing effect on a matte coating?

The writing effect is a visible line left when a fingernail, coin, key or other hard object is drawn across a matte film. The mark can be lighter or darker than the surrounding surface and may partly wipe off. It is caused by material transferred from the object, flattened surface peaks, or micro-damage in the film around the silica particles.

2. Why do matte coatings show fingernail marks more than gloss coatings?

A matte surface is deliberately rough on a micrometre scale. Protruding silica particles act like fine abrasive points that can scrape material off a nail or a metal object, and the textured surface holds that material. Any local flattening of the roughness also changes gloss, so the mark is easy to see. A smooth gloss film has neither effect to the same degree.

3. How can I tell transferred material from real film damage?

Wipe the mark gently with a soft, damp cloth or an eraser and inspect again under low-angle light. If it disappears, it was mostly transferred material. If a glossy or white line remains, the surface peaks were flattened or the film was damaged, and binder hardness, cure and silica choice need attention.

4. Does wax-treated silica reduce the writing effect?

In general industry experience, yes. The wax layer on the particle surface lowers friction so a nail or metal object slides instead of grating. Combining a wax-treated, fine, narrow-distribution silica with a small amount of slip or wax additive is a common starting point.

5. Will adding more slip additive always fix fingernail marks?

No. Slip additives help, but overdosing can cause cratering, a greasy feel or poor recoat adhesion. Add in small steps, test intercoat adhesion, and fix root causes such as soft binder, incomplete cure or coarse protruding particles at the same time.

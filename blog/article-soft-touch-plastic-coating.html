Soft-Touch Plastic Coatings: Choosing the Right Matting Agent

Published: 2026-11-17 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Plastic coatings

A soft touch plastic coating is judged by the hand before it is judged by the gloss meter. The customer picks up the phone case, remote control or door-trim panel and expects a dry, velvety, low-gloss surface that does not feel sticky, does not polish to a shine where the thumb rests, and does not mark with fingerprints. The binder creates most of that feel, but the matting agent decides whether the surface reads as premium matte or as rubbery and blotchy. This guide explains how soft-touch coatings work, what the matting agent does in them, how to choose and dose a grade, and how to test the result before it goes to the spray line.

It is written for formulators of waterborne soft-touch coatings for consumer electronics, automotive interiors, appliances and cosmetics packaging, and for buyers who need to understand why a grade that works in a hard matte topcoat may fail in a soft one.

How soft-touch plastic coatings work

Soft-touch feel comes from two things working together: a low-modulus, elastic film and a finely structured surface. The film gives slightly under the fingertip; the surface structure reduces the true contact area so the coating feels dry and velvety rather than tacky.

  • The binder. Soft-touch coatings are commonly based on two-component polyurethanes or waterborne polyurethane dispersions (PUDs), built with flexible polyols (polyester, polyether or polycarbonate types) so the cured film has a low glass transition temperature and an elastic response. Waterborne systems often blend a soft PUD with a harder dispersion or a crosslinker to balance feel against resistance.
  • The surface texture. Fine particles at the surface break up specular reflection, lowering gloss, and change how the skin slides across the film. Silica matting agents and organic matting beads (for example polyurethane or acrylic beads) are both used, often in combination.
  • Surface additives. Silicone or wax additives tune slip, which strongly affects perceived softness, and can change fingerprint visibility and recoatability.

Because the film is soft, it is mechanically more forgiving to the touch but also more vulnerable: it can feel tacky in heat, swell in contact with hand creams and sunscreens, and polish where it is repeatedly rubbed. The matting agent influences all of these.

What the matting agent does in a soft-touch film

In a hard matte topcoat, silica has one main job: create surface roughness that scatters light. In a soft-touch coating it has four:

  1. Lower and stabilise gloss. Most soft-touch specifications are in the matte to deep-matte range, frequently single-digit gloss units at 60°. A soft binder flows readily during drying, which tends to bury particles and raise gloss, so the matting agent must be efficient enough to keep the surface structured.
  2. Take away tack. Particles at the surface reduce the contact area between skin and the soft polymer, which is a large part of the "dry" feel that distinguishes soft-touch from simply sticky.
  3. Resist polishing. Rubbing flattens surface peaks; if the peaks are soft binder, the area turns glossy quickly. Well-anchored silica particles and wax treatment help the surface keep its structure.
  4. Even out appearance. Soft-touch parts are often dark (black, graphite, deep grey), where uneven matting shows as mottling and where poor transparency of the matting agent shows as a grey or whitish cast.

The trade-off is that silica is hard and porous. Too much of it, or particles that are too coarse, and the surface starts to feel dry and slightly abrasive instead of soft, and the film can lose flexibility and adhesion. The goal is the minimum effective dose of the right particle size.

Choosing a grade: silica, organic beads, or both

Formulators usually build the surface from one or two matting components. The comparison below reflects general formulation practice:

Matting componentStrengths in soft-touchWatch-outs
Fine silica (d50 around 4–5 μm)Smooth, dense micro-texture; good transparency; fine feelLower matting efficiency; may not reach deep matte at practical dosage
Medium wax-treated silica (d50 around 6–7 μm)High matting efficiency; dry, uniform feel; better scratch and burnish resistance; soft sedimentMust stay well below the dry film thickness to avoid grit
Coarse silica (d50 above 8 μm)Very low gloss at low dosageCan feel sandy and show texture; usually too rough for premium soft-touch
Organic beads (PU, acrylic)Elastic, rubbery feel; low gloss in thicker filmsLarger particle sizes; can settle or float; matting efficiency per gram is usually lower than porous silica

A common approach is to let organic beads or the soft binder deliver the cushion, and to use a wax-treated silica of medium particle size to set the gloss and the dry, silky surface. Where only silica is used, the choice of particle size relative to film build matters even more. Soft-touch coatings are often applied thicker than hard plastic topcoats, which gives more room for a medium-size grade, but the top cut of the distribution still decides whether you see seeds. Formulation guidelines

  1. Fix the substrate system first. Soft-touch films are usually applied over a primer or base coat on PC, ABS, PC/ABS or PP. Confirm adhesion of the unmatted soft-touch layer before adding silica, so you know any later adhesion loss is caused by the matting change.
  2. Run a dosage ladder. Prepare the same base at 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% matting agent on total formulation. Measure 60° and 85° gloss, and rate feel blind against your reference. Feel often has an optimum: below it the surface is tacky, above it the surface turns dry and slightly harsh.
  3. Keep the particle below the film. As a general rule, choose a d50 comfortably below the dry film thickness and ask for the d90 or full distribution curve. Oversize particles are the usual cause of grit on a soft surface, where they are easy to feel.
  4. Watch viscosity and solids. Porous silica absorbs liquid and builds viscosity. In waterborne soft-touch formulations this can push application viscosity up and solids down; adjust the rheology package rather than cutting the matting level.
  5. Tune slip after matting. Silicone and wax additives strongly change perceived softness. Settle the matting level first, then adjust slip, otherwise you are changing two variables at once.
  6. Balance crosslinking against feel. More crosslinker or a harder co-binder usually improves chemical and scratch resistance but reduces softness. The matting agent cannot compensate for a binder that is too hard or too soft; set the binder balance, then fine-tune with silica.
  7. Control drying. Slow, humid drying lets the soft binder flow over the particles and raises gloss. Keep flash-off, oven temperature and airflow consistent, and record them in trials.

Dispersion and application tips

Modern silica matting agents are supplied micronised, so the job in production is to wet the powder and break soft agglomerates, not to grind it. Over-milling reduces particle size and matting efficiency.

  • Add the silica gradually into the liquid phase under moderate stirring, then disperse at high speed for a fixed time. Keep speed and time identical batch to batch; our note on dispersing matting agents without bead milling gives a practical routine.
  • Check fineness on a Hegman gauge (ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210) and filter before spraying. Soft-touch parts are inspected closely, and a few agglomerates are enough to reject a part.
  • For 2K systems, add the crosslinker shortly before use and check gloss at the start and end of the pot life, as gloss and feel can drift as the mix ages.
  • Spray at a consistent film build. In soft-touch work, a few microns of difference in dry film thickness can change both gloss and feel.

Lab test plan for soft-touch plastic coatings

Feel is subjective, so pair panel ratings with measurable tests. A compact plan:

PropertyTypical methodWhat to look for
Gloss60° and 85° per ISO 2813 / ASTM D523Target met at nominal film build; low sensitivity to thinner and thicker films
HapticsBlind panel rating against a reference partDry, velvety, no tack, no grit; consistent between operators
Burnish and polishRub test (cloth or abrader), gloss before and afterSmall gloss rise in rubbed areas
AdhesionCross-hatch per ISO 2409 / ASTM D3359No loss at the highest matting level, before and after humidity exposure
Chemical resistanceSpot tests with agents named by the customer (hand cream, sunscreen, cleaners)No softening, tack, swelling or gloss change after recovery
AgeingHeat and humidity exposure as required by the specificationFeel and gloss retained; no stickiness developing over time
Storage stabilityOven storage of the liquid paint (e.g. 40–50 °C for several weeks)Soft, stir-back sediment; stable viscosity and gloss

Hydrolysis and chemical attack are known weak points of some soft-touch binders, so long-term ageing results are usually driven by the binder choice rather than the matting agent. The matting agent's role is to keep gloss, feel and appearance stable while the binder does its job.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What does a matting agent do in a soft-touch plastic coating?

It lowers and stabilises gloss, reduces tack by limiting the contact area between skin and the soft binder, helps the surface resist polishing where it is rubbed, and evens out appearance on dark parts. The soft feel itself comes mainly from the binder.

2. Which particle size works best for soft-touch coatings?

A medium, wax-treated silica with a d50 comfortably below the dry film thickness is a common starting point for deep-matte soft-touch, with a fine grade where smoothness matters more than very low gloss. Check the d90 or full distribution, because oversize particles are easy to feel on a soft surface.

3. Can I use silica and organic matting beads together?

Yes, and it is common. Organic beads can add an elastic, rubbery feel in thicker films, while porous silica sets the gloss efficiently and gives a dry, silky surface. Evaluate the blend with a dosage ladder in your own base.

4. Why does my soft-touch coating turn glossy where it is handled?

Repeated rubbing flattens surface peaks, and on a soft binder this polishes quickly. Check matting level, particle anchoring, wax or slip additives and binder hardness, and measure gloss before and after a rub test to compare options.

5. How much matting agent should a soft-touch coating contain?

It depends on binder, film build and gloss target. Censilcoat grades are used at 1–4% on total formulation. Run a 1/2/3/4% ladder and rate feel as well as gloss, because too much silica makes the surface feel dry and harsh and can reduce flexibility and adhesion.

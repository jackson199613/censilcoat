How to Get a True Matte Finish With Waterborne Wood Coatings

Published: 2026-11-12 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Wood coatings

A true waterborne matte wood finish is one that reads low-gloss from every viewing angle, stays clear over the grain, feels smooth and keeps its sheen after rubbing, storage and months of use. Getting there with a water-based system takes more than adding extra silica: the matting agent's particle size, the dry film thickness, the binder, the dispersion step and the drying conditions all move the final gloss. This guide walks through each of those levers, with practical ranges, a test checklist and a troubleshooting table for when the panel still looks too shiny, too grey or patchy.

It is written for formulators developing waterborne furniture, cabinet, door and flooring topcoats, and for technical buyers who need to understand why two "matte" products from different suppliers behave so differently on the line.

What "true matte" means for a waterborne wood finish

There is no single global definition of matte. Brands and furniture makers set their own gloss bands, usually measured at 60° according to ISO 2813 or ASTM D523. The working bands below are common in the wood-coatings industry, but always confirm the target and geometry with the customer's specification.

DescriptionTypical 60° gloss (GU)What to watch
Dead matte / full matteBelow about 10Clarity loss, rough feel, burnishing, high silica load
MatteAbout 10–20Sheen at low viewing angles, gloss drift in storage
Semi-matte / satinAbout 20–40Patchy sheen, sensitivity to film thickness
Semi-glossAbout 40–70Usually achieved with low silica loadings

The word "true" matters because many finishes hit their 60° number but still look shiny when you view a tabletop at a low angle. That is "sheen", and it is measured at 85°. A finish that reads well under 10 GU at 60° can still give a much higher 85° reading if the surface roughness is not fine and uniform enough. For matte wood finishes, specify and report both angles.

Why waterborne wood coatings are harder to matt

Silica matts a film because the binder shrinks around non-shrinking particles as water leaves, leaving micro-bumps on the surface that scatter light. (Our explainer on how a silica matting agent works covers the mechanism in detail.) Waterborne wood coatings add some complications:

  • Film formation by coalescence. Acrylic and polyurethane dispersions form a film when polymer particles pack and fuse. If coalescence is slow or incomplete, the surface stays mobile longer and silica particles can be wetted over by binder, which raises gloss.
  • Drying speed varies with humidity. Water evaporation slows sharply at high relative humidity. Slower, uneven drying gives more time for levelling and for particles to sink away from the surface, so gloss can differ between a dry winter line and a humid summer line. High humidity can also cause whitening; see whitening in waterborne matte coatings.
  • Low viscosity in the can. Spray-viscosity waterborne topcoats give dense particles an easy path to settle. Untreated silica can form a hard sediment that is never fully redispersed, so the applied coating contains less effective matting agent than the formula says.
  • Clarity expectations. Wood finishes are mostly clear. Extra silica or coarse particles that would be invisible in a pigmented paint show up as a grey veil over dark timber.

Choosing the right silica matting agent

The silica grade sets the ceiling for what the rest of the formulation can achieve. Four properties do most of the work:

  • Particle size (d50) relative to dry film thickness. Coarser particles create taller surface bumps and matt more efficiently, but too large a particle relative to film build shows as texture and grit. Wood topcoats typically run thicker than plastic coatings, so medium grades around 5–7 μm are a common starting point for furniture and cabinet topcoats.
  • Particle size distribution. A narrow distribution with few oversize particles keeps clarity and avoids "seeds". Ask suppliers for d90 or a full distribution curve, not only d50.
  • Pore volume. Higher pore volume means more particles per gram and better matting efficiency, at the price of more viscosity build and binder demand.
  • Surface treatment. Wax-treated grades generally give softer, stir-back sediment, a smoother hand-feel and better resistance to burnishing than untreated grades.

Formulation levers beyond the silica

When a panel is still too glossy, adding more silica is the first reflex and often the wrong one. Check these levers first:

  1. Run a dosage ladder, don't guess. Make the same base with, for example, 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% matting agent on total formulation and plot 60° and 85° gloss against dosage. The curve flattens at higher loadings; past that point, more silica mainly costs clarity and viscosity.
  2. Binder hardness and minimum film-forming temperature. Softer, slower-coalescing binders tend to flow over particles and give higher gloss at the same dosage than harder binders. If you change the binder blend, re-run the gloss curve.
  3. Coalescent and co-solvent level. Extra coalescent lengthens the open time and can raise gloss. Use the minimum needed for good film formation at your lowest application temperature.
  4. Rheology package. Some associative thickeners interact with silica surfaces and change viscosity or stability over time. Test viscosity at 24 hours and after oven storage, not only immediately.
  5. Wax and slip additives. Wax emulsions can support matting and improve burnish and scratch resistance, but they can also affect recoat adhesion. Check intercoat adhesion if the topcoat will be sanded and recoated.
  6. Solids and film build. Matting depends on how much the film shrinks and on the final dry film thickness. A higher-solids version of the same formula, or a thicker applied coat, often comes out glossier.

Dispersion and process: get the silica working

Modern matting silicas arrive already micronised. The job in the plant is to wet the powder and break up loose agglomerates, not to grind it. Over-milling can reduce particle size and lower matting efficiency.

  • Charge water, dispersing additive and defoamer, then add the silica slowly under moderate stirring to avoid lumps and dust.
  • Disperse on a high-speed disperser for a fixed time and speed, and keep both the same between batches; changing dispersion energy is a common cause of batch-to-batch gloss variation.
  • Check fineness on a Hegman gauge (ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210) to confirm there are no oversize agglomerates.
  • Let down with binder at lower speed and add thickeners last.
  • Many plants make a silica pre-paste or slurry and post-add it, which makes final gloss adjustment easier.

Application and drying: where matte finishes are won or lost

The same coating can measure very differently depending on how it is applied. Points to standardise on the line:

  • Dry film thickness per coat. Keep it within the range you used in the lab. Heavy coats generally give higher gloss and more haze.
  • Matte only where it is needed. In a sealer-plus-topcoat system, the topcoat controls the final gloss. Heavily matting the sealer adds haze under the topcoat without much benefit; many finishers use a lightly matted or unmatted sealer and a fully matted topcoat.
  • Drying conditions. Temperature, airflow and humidity in the flash-off zone and drying room all shift gloss. Record them alongside gloss readings so you can separate formulation effects from process effects.
  • Spray settings. Atomisation, fluid pressure and gun distance influence film build and surface uniformity. Patchy sheen on large panels often traces back to uneven overlap rather than the coating.

Testing checklist for a waterborne matte wood finish

  • Gloss at 60° and 85° on a defined substrate, film thickness and drying schedule.
  • Clarity over a black panel and over dark wood such as walnut, compared side by side with the reference.
  • Burnish and rub resistance: rub with a cloth or abrasive pad and measure the gloss increase.
  • Storage stability: oven storage (a common choice is 40–50 °C for several weeks), then check sediment type, redispersion, viscosity and gloss drift.
  • Humidity sensitivity: apply and dry at high relative humidity and look for whitening.
  • Feel and scratch as required by the end use (fingernail marks, writing effect, stain resistance).

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How do I get a matte finish with a waterborne wood coating?

Use a silica matting agent sized to your dry film thickness, find the right loading with a dosage ladder, disperse it consistently on a high-speed disperser, keep film build and drying conditions controlled, and measure gloss at both 60° and 85°. Put most of the matting in the topcoat rather than the sealer to protect clarity.

2. Why is my waterborne matte finish still shiny?

Common causes are a grade that is too fine for the film thickness, too low a dosage, heavy film build, a soft or slow-coalescing binder, too much coalescent, slow drying at high humidity, or hard-settled silica that never got back into the coating. Measure 85° gloss as well as 60° to see whether the issue is low-angle sheen.

3. How much matting agent does a waterborne wood topcoat need?

It depends on the grade, binder, film thickness and gloss target. Censilcoat grades are used at 1–4% on total formulation. Run a ladder such as 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% in your own base and plot gloss against dosage; if you need much more than that, a more efficient grade is usually the better fix.

4. Should the sealer be matted too?

Usually only lightly, if at all. The topcoat sets the final gloss, while silica in the sealer adds haze under the topcoat, especially on dark wood. Many finishers use a clear or lightly matted sealer and a fully matted topcoat.

5. Why does my matte wood finish turn glossy where it is rubbed?

That is burnishing: rubbing flattens or polishes the exposed surface roughness. Wax-treated silica, a wax additive and a harder binder improve burnish resistance. Test by rubbing a cured panel and measuring the gloss increase.

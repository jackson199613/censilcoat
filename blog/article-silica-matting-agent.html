What Is a Silica Matting Agent and How Does It Work in Coatings?

Published: 2026-11-12 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Core product

A silica matting agent is a fine, porous, synthetic amorphous silica powder that formulators add to a coating to lower its gloss. Once the film dries, the silica particles roughen the surface on a micrometre scale, so incoming light is scattered in many directions instead of being reflected like a mirror. The result is a satin, matte or dead-matte look, while a well-chosen grade keeps a clear coat transparent because silica's refractive index sits close to that of most binders.

This guide explains what the material is, the physics behind the gloss reduction, which datasheet parameters actually predict performance, how to add and test it, and where it fits in waterborne wood and plastic coatings. It is written for R&D chemists setting up a matte formulation and for buyers who need to understand what they are comparing between suppliers.

What a silica matting agent is (and what it isn't)

Commercial matting silicas are almost always synthetic amorphous silica (SiO₂). Two production routes dominate:

  • Precipitated silica — made by reacting sodium silicate with acid in water, then filtering, washing, drying and milling. The process gives porous aggregates whose pore volume and particle size can be tuned. It is the workhorse of waterborne matting.
  • Silica gel — made by forming a continuous gel that is then washed, dried and milled. Gels tend to have very regular porosity and are widely used in clear coats.

Both are milled and air-classified to a median particle size (d50) of a few micrometres. Many grades are then given a surface treatment — commonly a wax (for example polyethylene wax) or an organic coating — that improves soft settling, film feel and scratch resistance.

Two materials are often confused with matting silica. Fumed silica has nanometre-scale primary particles and is used mainly for rheology and anti-settling, not matting. Crystalline silica (quartz, cristobalite) is a different material with a different hazard profile; matting agents are amorphous. Our explainer on amorphous silica covers that distinction in detail.

How a silica matting agent works: the mechanism

Gloss is the fraction of light reflected at the specular angle. A smooth film reflects most light specularly and looks glossy. To make it look matte, you need surface irregularities large enough to scatter visible light — roughly in the range of a fraction of a micrometre up to a few micrometres in height.

1. Film shrinkage pushes particles to the surface

A wet film is mostly water or solvent plus binder. As the volatiles leave, the film shrinks dramatically in thickness. Silica particles do not shrink, so as the binder layer contracts around them, the particles end up forming bumps on the surface. The greater the shrinkage, the more pronounced the roughness — which is one reason waterborne systems, with relatively low solids and high shrinkage, generally matt efficiently.

2. Micro-roughness scatters light

Those bumps break up the specular reflection. Coarser particles create taller bumps and more scattering per particle, so they matt more efficiently, but they also make the surface feel rougher and can show as visible texture or "seeds" in thin films. Finer particles give a smoother touch and better clarity but need a higher loading for the same gloss.

3. Refractive-index matching keeps clears transparent

Amorphous silica has a refractive index of roughly 1.45–1.46, close to typical acrylic and polyurethane binders. Inside the film the particles are therefore nearly invisible. Haze in a matte clear usually comes from particles that are too large, too many, poorly dispersed, or surrounded by air or water voids — not from the silica itself.

4. Porosity sets the efficiency

Porous particles have a low apparent density, so one gram of a high-pore-volume silica contains many more particles than one gram of a dense filler. More particles per gram means more surface bumps per gram — higher matting efficiency at the same dosage. The trade-off is that porous particles absorb binder and water and can raise viscosity.

A useful rule of thumb from industry practice: match particle size to dry film thickness. Thin films (for example plastic coatings in the 10–25 μm range) usually need finer grades to avoid visible texture, while thicker wood topcoats can use coarser, more efficient grades. Silica vs other matting technologies

Silica is not the only way to reduce gloss. Formulators often combine it with other materials, so it helps to know where each one fits.

Wax matting additivesWax migrates to surface and crystallisesSmooth feel, slip, scratch resistanceLimited matting power alone; can affect recoat adhesion

In clear wood finishes and transparent plastic topcoats, silica is usually the primary matting agent because nothing else combines high efficiency with transparency. Wax or beads are added on top when the specification calls for a particular feel or burnish resistance.

How to add and disperse it

Matting silica is supplied already micronised. The job is to wet it and break up loose agglomerates, not to grind it further — heavy milling can reduce particle size and cut matting efficiency. A practical lab sequence:

  1. Charge water (and co-solvent if used) plus wetting/dispersing additive and defoamer.
  2. Add the silica slowly under moderate stirring to avoid dust and lumps.
  3. Disperse with a high-speed disperser (Censilcoat grades: 15–20 minutes, no bead mill required).
  4. Check fineness on a Hegman gauge (ISO 1524 / ASTM D1210) — you are looking for the absence of oversize agglomerates, not a "high" reading at any cost.
  5. Let down with the binder and remaining additives at lower speed; add thickeners last and adjust viscosity.

Alternatively, many plants prepare a silica pre-paste or slurry and post-add it, which makes gloss adjustment between batches easier.

Testing a matte formulation: a short checklist

  • Gloss at 60° and 85° on a defined substrate and film thickness (ISO 2813 / ASTM D523). For low-gloss finishes, the 85° reading separates samples that look similar at 60°.
  • Clarity — draw down over a black panel or dark wood and compare haze side by side with your reference.
  • Viscosity before and after silica addition, and after 24 hours.
  • Storage stability — oven storage (a common choice is 40–50 °C for several weeks), then check sediment type, re-dispersion and gloss drift.
  • Surface resistance — burnish/rub, scratch and stain tests appropriate to the end use.
  • Humidity sensitivity — for waterborne systems, apply and dry at high relative humidity to look for whitening.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a silica matting agent made of?

A silica matting agent is synthetic amorphous silicon dioxide, usually precipitated silica or silica gel, milled and classified to a few micrometres. Many grades carry a surface treatment such as wax or an organic coating that improves settling behaviour, film feel and scratch resistance. It is amorphous, not crystalline silica such as quartz.

2. How much silica matting agent should I add?

It depends on the gloss target, film thickness, binder and the grade's pore volume. For Censilcoat grades the working range is 1–4% on total formulation. Run a ladder (for example 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%) in your own system and plot 60° gloss against dosage to find the level that hits your target.

3. Why does my matte clear coat look hazy or grey?

Common causes are overdosing, a grade that is too coarse or has a wide particle size distribution, poor dispersion that leaves agglomerates, and incomplete film coalescence at high humidity. Check on a black panel or dark wood, verify Hegman fineness, and test a finer, narrow-distribution grade at the same gloss target.

4. Is fumed silica a matting agent?

Not in practice. Fumed silica has very small primary particles that build a network in the liquid, so it is mainly used for rheology and anti-settling. Matting needs particles in the micrometre range that create surface roughness, which is why precipitated silica and silica gel are the usual matting materials.

5. Do silica matting agents need bead milling?

Matting-grade silica is supplied already micronised, so the goal is to wet and de-agglomerate it, not to grind it. Censilcoat grades are designed for high-speed dispersion for 15–20 minutes without bead milling. Heavy milling can reduce particle size and lower matting efficiency.

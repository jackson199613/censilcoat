Precipitated vs Fumed Silica Matting Agents for Waterborne Coatings

Published: 2026-11-20 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Core product

If you are choosing a precipitated silica matting agent for a waterborne coating, you will quickly meet the other big family of synthetic amorphous silica: fumed (also called pyrogenic or thermal) silica. Both are chemically almost pure SiO₂, both are amorphous, and both appear in waterborne formulas. But they are made in completely different ways, they have different particle structures, and in most waterborne wood and plastic coatings they do different jobs. Precipitated silica, milled and classified to a few microns, is the workhorse for reducing gloss. Fumed silica is mostly used in small amounts for rheology and anti-settling, with a few specially processed thermal grades sold for matting. This guide explains the difference, when each one makes sense, and how to test them side by side in your own base.

Two routes to the same chemistry

The difference between the two families starts in the reactor.

Precipitated silica: a wet process

Precipitated silica is made in water. A sodium silicate solution (water glass) is reacted with an acid, usually sulfuric acid, under controlled temperature, pH and concentration. Silica nuclei form, grow into primary particles and then link into porous aggregates and agglomerates. The slurry is filtered, washed to remove salts, dried (often by spray drying) and then milled and classified to the target particle size. For matting grades, the last steps matter most: milling and classification set the median particle size (d50) and the top size, and an optional surface treatment, such as a wax or organic coating, is applied before or during milling.

Because the structure is built in the wet stage, pore volume and surface area can be tuned through reaction conditions, so the same chemistry can be engineered for clarity, matting efficiency or feel.

Fumed silica: a flame process

Fumed silica is made by burning a volatile silicon compound, typically silicon tetrachloride, in a hydrogen and oxygen flame. The silicon compound is converted to SiO₂ in the gas phase, where tiny non-porous primary particles, in the range of roughly 5–50 nm, fuse into branched, chain-like aggregates. The product is an extremely light, fluffy powder with very low bulk density and a high external surface area, often anywhere from about 50 to 400 m²/g depending on grade. Hydrophilic grades carry silanol groups on the surface; hydrophobic grades are after-treated with silanes or siloxanes.

Those branched aggregates are the reason fumed silica is famous as a thickener: in liquids they form a hydrogen-bonded network that gives thixotropy and holds other particles in suspension. Some thermal silicas are further processed into larger, compacted particles specifically for matting, and these are sold as matting agents in their own right.

Structure: why the particles behave differently

Gloss reduction comes from surface roughness. As a waterborne film dries and shrinks, matting particles of a few microns sit near the surface and create micro-roughness that scatters reflected light. To do this well, a matting agent needs particles in the right size range, enough of them per unit of film, and good wetting by the binder so they do not add haze.

  • Precipitated matting grades are porous micron-sized particles, commonly with d50 values somewhere between about 3 and 10 μm. Their internal pores make each particle light, so a given weight contains many particles. This is why pore volume is one of the main drivers of matting efficiency.
  • Standard fumed silica is made of nanometre-scale aggregates. Well dispersed, those aggregates are far too small to roughen a film surface the way a 5 μm particle does, so ordinary fumed grades are poor matting agents at normal loadings. What they do very efficiently is build structure in the liquid.
  • Thermal matting grades are fumed silica that has been processed into micron-sized particles. They can matt well and are used in some demanding systems, but their structure, surface chemistry and handling differ from precipitated grades, so a direct swap needs testing.

How each behaves in a waterborne formula

Matting efficiency and gloss curve

Matting efficiency is the gloss reduction you get per percent of matting agent at a given film thickness. In precipitated silica it is controlled mainly by particle size and pore volume: coarser and more porous particles matt more strongly, finer particles give a smoother feel and higher clarity at the cost of needing more dosage. Run a dosage ladder, for example 1%, 2%, 3% and 4%, and measure 60° and 85° gloss on the same dry film thickness to see the full curve. If a grade needs much more than about 5% to reach target, the usual answer is a different grade, not more powder.

Viscosity and rheology

Every silica raises viscosity, but by different mechanisms. Porous precipitated particles absorb part of the liquid phase into their pores, so viscosity climbs with dosage and pore volume. Fumed silica builds a network between aggregates, giving pronounced shear thinning and recovery. In waterborne systems, pH, surfactants and associative thickeners can all change how fumed silica performs, so a combined formula is not always the sum of its parts. Measure viscosity at low and high shear after 24 hours, not only straight after mixing.

Settling and storage stability

This is where the two families often work together. Precipitated matting particles are denser than the surrounding liquid and will settle over time in a low-viscosity waterborne base. Untreated particles can pack into a hard cake that is difficult to stir back. A wax-treated precipitated grade tends to form a softer, looser sediment. A small addition of hydrophilic fumed silica can add enough low-shear structure to slow settling further. Our article on eliminating hard sediment in waterborne matte coatings covers this combination and typical starting levels in detail.

Clarity and appearance

For clear coats on dark wood, clarity depends on particle size distribution (oversized particles scatter light and look grey), on how well the binder wets the silica, and on how closely the refractive index of the silica matches the dried binder. A narrow distribution with a controlled top size is usually more important than the median value alone. Fumed silica used for rheology can add slight haze at higher levels, so keep it to the minimum needed for stability.

When to use precipitated, fumed, or both

A simple decision guide for waterborne wood and plastic coatings:

  1. Your main goal is lower gloss. Start with a precipitated silica matting agent selected by target gloss, film thickness and clarity requirement.
  2. The matte formula settles or hard-packs in storage. First try a wax-treated precipitated grade; if long storage in a very thin base is still a problem, add a small amount of hydrophilic fumed silica or another anti-settling agent and recheck viscosity.
  3. You are replacing an existing thermal matting grade. A precipitated grade can often match gloss and feel, but plan to re-check viscosity, settling and touch because the structure and surface treatment differ. Our guide to a TS 100 alternative for deep-matte plastic coatings works through this case.
  4. You need the highest clarity in a water-white clear coat. Choose a precipitated grade with a narrow particle size distribution and keep any rheology additive at the lowest effective level.

A lab checklist for comparing silica grades

Whether you are comparing a precipitated silica matting agent with a fumed or thermal grade, or two precipitated grades, keep the method constant so that differences come from the material.

  • Use the same base, the same addition point and the same dispersion time and speed for every sample.
  • Record Hegman fineness, then viscosity at low and high shear after 24 hours.
  • Apply at a controlled wet film thickness and record dry film thickness.
  • Measure 60° and 85° gloss on each panel, several readings per panel.
  • Check clarity over a black panel or dark-stained wood, and assess feel by touch.
  • Store sealed samples, commonly 7 days at 50 °C, then check sediment hardness, re-stir and re-measure gloss.
  • Repeat with a second lot before approving a new material.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the difference between precipitated and fumed silica?

Precipitated silica is made in water by reacting sodium silicate with acid, then dried, milled and classified into porous micron-sized particles. Fumed silica is made by burning a silicon compound in a flame, giving non-porous nanometre particles fused into branched aggregates.

2. Which is better for matting waterborne coatings?

For most waterborne wood and plastic coatings, a precipitated silica matting agent is the usual choice for gloss reduction. Standard fumed silica is mainly used in small amounts for rheology and anti-settling, although some specially processed thermal grades are sold for matting.

3. Can I use fumed and precipitated silica together?

Yes. A common approach is to let a precipitated grade do the matting and add a small amount of hydrophilic fumed silica for low-shear structure that slows settling. Check viscosity, gloss and clarity after adding it.

4. Why does pore volume matter in a precipitated silica matting agent?

Higher pore volume makes each particle lighter, so the same weight contains more particles and usually matts more efficiently. It also affects viscosity, so it has to be balanced against the rheology of the base.

5. Can a precipitated grade replace a thermal matting silica?

Often it can match gloss and feel, but the structure and surface treatment differ, so re-check viscosity, settling, clarity and touch in your own system. Censil3851 is benchmarked against TS 100 for this purpose.

TS 100 Alternative for Deep-Matte Plastic Coatings

Published: 2026-11-19 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Sourcing

Looking for a TS 100 alternative for deep matte plastic coatings usually starts with price or lead time, but you only finish the job by matching the finish itself. TS 100 is widely used as a reference grade for very low gloss: a coarse, highly efficient silica that pushes 60° gloss into single figures on thin films. A replacement has to reach the same gloss at a similar dosage and keep the surface hard, smooth and even, without thickening the paint or leaving specks on a 15–25 μm film. This guide explains what to match, what changes when you move from a thermal silica to a precipitated one, and how to qualify the change in the lab before it reaches the spray line.

Why TS 100 became the deep-matte reference

Deep matte is usually taken to mean roughly 10 GU and below at 60°, often specified together with an 85° reading because the 60° geometry loses resolution at very low gloss. Getting there needs many particles large enough to roughen the dried film surface, and enough of them per square centimetre that very little flat binder is left between them.

TS 100 does this with a relatively coarse particle size. Its manufacturer publishes a d50 of 9.5 μm and a nitrogen surface area of about 250 m²/g for an untreated thermal (pyrogenic) silica, as summarised with sources on our TS 100 alternative benchmark page. Because the surface is untreated, the grade is easy to wet in many systems and very efficient per kilogram. That combination is why many plastic-coating formulators specify it for automotive interior parts, consumer-electronics housings and other hard, tactile surfaces where the finish must look dead flat.

The same properties set the targets for any substitute: comparable coarse particle size, high porosity for efficiency, and a clean upper tail in the size distribution so the film stays smooth.

Why deep matte is harder on plastic substrates

Plastic coatings are usually applied thinner than wood coatings, often 10–25 μm dry for topcoats on ABS, PC/ABS or PP primers. Thin films change the matting problem in several ways:

  • Particle size versus film thickness. In general, a matting particle needs to be a meaningful fraction of the dry film thickness to protrude and scatter light. A 9 μm particle in a 15 μm film behaves very differently from the same particle in a 40 μm wood topcoat. Too fine and the gloss floor is too high; too coarse and the surface feels rough and the coarse tail shows as seeds.
  • Burnish and polish. Deep-matte plastic parts are handled, wiped and rubbed. Protruding silica on a soft binder can be pressed flat, leaving glossy patches. Binder hardness and particle anchoring matter as much as gloss on the day of spraying.
  • Viscosity window. Spray-applied plastic topcoats run at low application viscosity. High silica loadings build viscosity and structure, which can affect atomisation and levelling.
  • Uniformity. At 3–6 GU, small changes in film thickness, flash-off or oven airflow show up as visible sheen differences across a part or between parts.

Our broader grade selection guide for plastic coatings covers the general case. Here the focus is the extreme low-gloss end, where a TS 100 type grade is normally chosen.

Thermal vs precipitated silica: what changes when you switch

Most alternatives to TS 100 on the market are precipitated silicas, because precipitation is a flexible, widely used route for porous matting silica. TS 100 is described by its maker as a thermal silica. Both are synthetic amorphous silica, and both can deliver very low gloss, but the switch is not purely cosmetic. In general terms, formulators should expect differences in these areas:

Surface treatment and settling

An untreated silica settles more readily into compact sediment in low-viscosity waterborne systems, which is why many formulations using one rely on anti-settling rheology. Wax-modified precipitated grades usually form a softer sediment that stirs back more easily. If your current formula carries extra anti-settling additive only because of the silica, a treated alternative may let you reduce it later. Do not change both at once during qualification.

Touch and scratch behaviour

Wax treatment tends to give a smoother, slightly slippery feel and can help with mar resistance, because the wax sits at the particle surface where contact happens. It can also change how the film responds to fingernail marks and polishing. Evaluate touch, burnish and scratch on cured panels rather than assuming they will match.

Rheology and wetting

Different surface chemistry interacts differently with associative thickeners, dispersants and wetting agents. Expect the viscosity curve to shift a little even at equal dosage, and check it at low and high shear.

Recoat and adhesion

For multi-coat plastic systems, confirm that the treated silica does not affect intercoat adhesion or recoatability. A cross-cut test after recoating within and beyond your normal process window is a sensible check.

None of these is a reason to avoid switching. They are the points to test deliberately, so the trial confirms the finish instead of surprising you on the line.

A lab protocol for qualifying a TS 100 alternative

A structured trial keeps the comparison fair and gives purchasing a clear decision. The steps below are a practical starting point that many coating labs adapt; our six-step guide to replacing imported matting agents 1:1 covers the commercial side.

  1. Fix the reference. Use your current production formula and a retained lot of TS 100. Record its gloss at 60° and 85°, viscosity, fineness and cured-film properties as the baseline.
  2. Run a dosage ladder. Prepare the candidate at your current dosage and at ±0.5% around it. Keep binder, additives, solids and dispersion conditions identical.
  3. Disperse the same way. High-speed dispersion of the same duration and tip speed for both grades. Record Hegman fineness so you know both are fully wetted and neither is over-ground.
  4. Apply at production film thickness. Spray onto the actual plastic substrate and primer, at your real dry film thickness, with the same flash and cure. Also spray at the low and high end of your thickness tolerance; deep-matte formulas often reveal their sensitivity there.
  5. Measure gloss properly. Read 60° and 85° at several points per panel and average them. At very low gloss, small differences in instrument geometry and panel flatness matter; see our guide on how to measure gloss on matte panels.
  6. Check appearance and feel. Look for specks, sheen uniformity and any grey or milky cast on dark colours. Do a blind touch comparison with two or three people.
  7. Test durability. Rub or burnish tests, fingernail and mar resistance, and any chemical or hand-cream resistance tests your customer specifies. Measure gloss change after rubbing, not just visual rating.
  8. Check stability. Store both paints at room temperature and at an elevated temperature such as 40–50 °C, then check settling, stir-back, viscosity and re-sprayed gloss.

Accept the candidate when gloss, appearance and durability fall inside the tolerance you already accept between lots of the reference. If the gloss is close but not equal, adjust dosage in small steps before considering formula changes.

Formulation tips for deep matte on plastics

Whichever silica you use, the formula around it decides how robust the deep-matte finish will be. These general guidelines help when targeting very low gloss:

  • Control film thickness. Gloss in deep-matte coatings usually rises as the film gets thicker, because more binder covers the particles. Tight film-build control is often the cheapest gloss-uniformity fix.
  • Combine grades for feel. A coarse grade sets the gloss floor; a portion of a finer grade can fill the gaps and smooth the touch. Small blends are a common way to balance gloss against feel.
  • Use binder hardness for burnish resistance. A harder or more crosslinked binder holds particles in place under rubbing. In soft-feel systems, accept a slightly higher gloss or add a wax additive to protect the surface.
  • Watch solids and shrinkage. Lower-solids formulas shrink more on drying, which helps expose particles and lowers gloss, but too low a solids content can cause poor film formation or mud cracking at high silica loadings.
  • Keep viscosity in the spray window. Add silica under good agitation and adjust thickener after the silica is fully wetted, not before.
  • Do not grind. Bead milling breaks porous silica aggregates and raises gloss. High-speed dispersion is normally enough; see our guide to dispersing matting agents without bead milling.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a good TS 100 alternative for deep matte coatings?

Look for a porous amorphous silica with a similar coarse median particle size, high pore volume, a tight upper size tail and a comparable recommended dosage. Censil3851 (d50 8.5–9.0 μm, pore volume 1.6–1.8 mL/g) is the grade Censilcoat benchmarks against TS 100, but any alternative should be confirmed in a side-by-side trial in your own formula.

2. Can a precipitated silica replace a thermal silica like TS 100?

Often yes for the matting result, but the surface chemistry differs, so test settling, rheology, touch, burnish resistance and recoat adhesion rather than assuming they match. Start at your current dosage and fine-tune from there.

3. What gloss level counts as deep matte?

There is no single standard definition, but deep matte is commonly taken as about 10 GU or less at 60°. Because the 60° geometry loses resolution at very low gloss, many specifications also state an 85° value.

4. Why does my deep-matte plastic coating turn glossy when rubbed?

This is burnishing: rubbing flattens or polishes the protruding silica and the binder between particles. A harder or more crosslinked binder, a wax additive or a wax-treated silica, and controlled film thickness usually improve it.

5. How long does it take to qualify a TS 100 alternative?

A lab comparison of gloss, appearance, durability and accelerated storage stability typically takes one to two weeks, followed by a line trial on production parts before full switchover.

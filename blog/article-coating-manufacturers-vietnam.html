Vietnam's Coatings Industry: What Formulators Need From Suppliers

Published: 2026-11-15 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Market & regulation

For coating manufacturers in Vietnam, the hard part of raw material sourcing is rarely finding a product on paper. It is finding a supplier whose material behaves the same way every batch, whose documents satisfy export customers, and who can get a sample and a technical answer to the lab before the next customer deadline. In short, Vietnamese formulators need four things from suppliers: consistent quality backed by clear COA data, grades that are benchmarked against the materials already in their formulas, fast and flexible supply in sensible quantities, and documentation that holds up when a furniture brand or importer asks questions. This article looks at why those needs are sharper in Vietnam than in many markets, and gives a practical checklist for qualifying suppliers, using silica matting agents as the worked example.

It is written for R&D managers who have to protect a finish that a customer has already approved, and for procurement teams who are asked to cut cost and supply risk without causing a single complaint on the line.

Where coating manufacturers in Vietnam sell

A large share of industrial coating demand in Vietnam is tied to manufacturing for export, and wood furniture is the clearest example. Vietnam's wood and wood product exports reached a record of more than US$17 billion in 2025, according to Viet Nam News. Every one of those chairs, tables and cabinets carries a finish, and most of those finishes are specified, at least in part, by the overseas buyer.

That shapes the coatings business in practical ways. The customer of a wood coating plant is often a furniture factory working to a brand's standard panel, gloss range and test list. Approvals take time, and once a finish is approved, nobody wants it to change. Alongside furniture, coating plants also serve domestic construction, metal fabrication and a growing base of plastic part and electronics assembly, each with its own requirements. For many formulators the result is a wide portfolio, frequent colour and gloss matches, and little tolerance for raw material surprises.

The technical pressures Vietnamese formulators face

Several conditions make coating formulation in Vietnam more demanding than a standard lab recipe suggests. None of these are unique to Vietnam, but they tend to occur together.

  • Heat and humidity. Much of the country has a hot, humid climate for long parts of the year. Waterborne films dry more slowly at high relative humidity, and matte clear coats are prone to whitening or blushing when coalescence is incomplete. Our article on whitening in waterborne matte coatings at high humidity explains the mechanisms.
  • The shift to waterborne. Export customers and brand owners increasingly ask for lower-VOC and waterborne systems. Converting from solvent-borne or nitrocellulose finishes changes how every additive behaves, including the matting agent, which must disperse and stay stable in a low-viscosity water phase.
  • Fast lines and short drying. Furniture finishing lines run on tight cycle times with flash-off zones and drying tunnels. Gloss in matte films depends on drying conditions, so a raw material that shifts gloss with temperature or airflow causes visible variation between shifts.
  • Many substrates and species. A single plant may finish rubberwood, acacia, imported oak and veneered panels, plus MDF. Clarity over dark or stained wood and consistent sheen across porous and closed surfaces are recurring challenges.
  • Demanding matte targets. Matte and dead-matte looks remain popular in furniture, and the lower the gloss target, the more sensitive the formula becomes to the particle size, pore volume and dosage of the matting agent.

What formulators need from raw material suppliers

When R&D and purchasing teams in coating plants describe a "good supplier", the list is remarkably consistent. It is less about the lowest price on a quotation and more about what happens after the first order.

  1. Batch-to-batch consistency. The second, tenth and fiftieth lot must give the same gloss, viscosity and clarity as the qualified sample. Ask how key properties are controlled and which ones are tested on every lot.
  2. A meaningful COA. A certificate of analysis should report the properties that actually move your finish, for a matting agent typically particle size, moisture or loss on drying, pH and a structural value such as oil absorption or pore volume, with stated limits and methods.
  3. Benchmark data against what you use today. Formulators do not want to rebuild a formula. A supplier who can show how its grade compares with your incumbent, and who expects you to verify that in your own system, saves weeks.
  4. Samples that arrive quickly. A trial cannot start without material. Lab-size samples with TDS and SDS, shipped promptly, keep qualification on schedule.
  5. Sensible minimum order quantities. Many plants use a few hundred kilograms of a specialty additive per month. A minimum order of several tons ties up cash and warehouse space and discourages second-source qualification.
  6. Short, predictable lead times. Long ocean routes, transhipment and distributor stock-outs force plants to hold safety stock. Regional supply shortens the planning horizon.
  7. Technical support in the right language and time zone. When a line shows haze or gloss drift on a Monday morning, a same-day answer from someone who understands waterborne formulation matters more than a brochure.
  8. Change notification. Any change to raw materials, process or plant that could affect performance should be notified before it reaches your warehouse.

Coating manufacturers in Vietnam typically buy specialty additives through one of three routes. Each has a place; many plants use more than one to spread risk. The comparison below is a general one and individual suppliers vary.

How to qualify a new supplier without risking the finish

A short, structured qualification protects both the formulator and the purchasing team. For a matting agent, a workable sequence looks like this:

  1. Screen documents first. Request TDS, SDS and a sample COA. Check that the material type is clearly stated (for silica matting agents, amorphous rather than crystalline silica) and that the COA covers the properties you care about.
  2. Run a side-by-side ladder. Test the candidate against your incumbent in the same base, at the same dosage and at least two steps either side, with identical dispersion and film build.
  3. Measure what the customer sees. Gloss at 60° and 85°, clarity over black or dark-stained wood, feel and burnish resistance, plus viscosity and Hegman fineness.
  4. Stress the system. Heat-age sealed samples, commonly 7 days at 50 °C, and apply panels at high humidity if your line experiences it.
  5. Check a second lot. Consistency only shows across lots. Compare results from at least two production lots before switching significant volume.
  6. Trial on the real line. A production batch on the normal spray line and drying tunnel is the final proof. Keep the incumbent as a dual source until performance on the line is confirmed.

The documentation export customers ask about

Because so much Vietnamese production is exported, coating plants increasingly receive questionnaires from furniture makers, importers and brand owners. Typical requests include current SDS documents, confirmation of regulatory status for the destination market, restricted-substance declarations and, for some brands, information on VOC content of the finished coating. A supplier who can provide accurate, consistent documents quickly makes these requests much easier to answer.

Documentation pressure on the wood itself is rising too. The EU Deforestation Regulation covers wood and many wood products, and its application date was postponed to December 2026, as noted by the European Commission's Access2Markets service. The regulation concerns the timber supply chain rather than coatings, but furniture makers preparing traceability files for EU buyers tend to expect the same orderliness from all their material suppliers. Check the current text with your own regulatory advisers before relying on any date.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What do coating manufacturers in Vietnam look for in a raw material supplier?

Mainly batch-to-batch consistency, a COA that reports the properties that affect the finish, benchmark data against the grade already in use, fast samples, sensible minimum order quantities, short lead times and responsive technical support.

2. Why is humidity a particular issue for waterborne matte coatings in Vietnam?

High relative humidity slows water evaporation and film coalescence. In matte clear coats this can show up as whitening or blushing, so formulators test panels under humid conditions and choose matting agents and coalescent packages that tolerate them.

3. How should a Vietnamese coating plant qualify a new matting agent?

Screen the TDS, SDS and COA first, then run a side-by-side dosage ladder against the incumbent with identical dispersion and film build. Measure 60° and 85° gloss, clarity, viscosity and fineness, heat-age the samples, check a second lot and finish with a production line trial.

4. Does the EU Deforestation Regulation apply to coatings?

The EUDR covers wood and many wood products rather than coatings themselves. Its application date was postponed to December 2026. Furniture exporters preparing traceability files often expect clear and consistent documentation from all of their suppliers, including coating and additive suppliers.

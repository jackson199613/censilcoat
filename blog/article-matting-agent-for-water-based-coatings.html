Choosing a Matting Agent for Water-Based Coatings: 7 Parameters That Matter

Published: 2026-11-28 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Core product

Choosing a matting agent for water based coatings comes down to seven measurable parameters: particle size distribution, pore volume, surface treatment, effect on rheology, optical clarity, dispersibility and storage stability. Get those seven right for your binder, film thickness and gloss target, and the grade will matte efficiently, stay clear on dark substrates and behave the same from the first drum to the last. Get one wrong and you see it as haze, specks, a viscosity jump, hard sediment or gloss that drifts between batches.

This guide explains what each parameter does, how to read it on a datasheet, how to test it in your own lab, and a simple selection checklist. It is written for R&D chemists formulating waterborne wood and plastic coatings, and for the purchasing teams who have to compare quotes for grades that look similar on paper.

Why waterborne systems are harder to matte than solvent-borne ones

Most matting agents used in water based coatings are amorphous silica, usually precipitated silica or silica gel, sometimes surface treated with wax. The mechanism is the same in any binder: as the film dries and shrinks, the silica particles create micro-roughness at the surface, and that roughness scatters light diffusely instead of reflecting it as a sharp image. Lower specular reflection means lower gloss.

Waterborne systems make this harder in three ways, which is why a grade that works well in a solvent-borne lacquer does not automatically work in an acrylic or polyurethane dispersion:

  • Film formation is different. Latex and PUD particles coalesce as water leaves, and film shrinkage is often lower than in a solvent-borne system with the same solids. Less shrinkage gives less surface relief, so you may need a slightly coarser or more porous grade, or a higher dosage, for the same gloss.
  • Films are often thin. Waterborne plastic coatings and some wood topcoats are applied at low dry film thickness. The matting agent has to be sized to the film, or the largest particles show through as grit.
  • The continuous phase is water. Untreated silica surfaces are hydrophilic and carry silanol groups. In water they interact with thickeners, surfactants and each other, which affects viscosity, foam and settling behaviour in the can.

Parameter 1: particle size distribution (d50 and top size)

Particle size is the first lever on gloss, clarity and surface feel. The median size, d50, tells you roughly where the grade sits; the top size (often reported as d90 or d97, or checked with a Hegman gauge) tells you whether you will see seeds in a thin film.

  • Finer grades (roughly 3–5 μm d50) give a smoother, silkier touch and better clarity, and suit thin films and high-clarity clear coats, but matte less efficiently per kilogram.
  • Medium grades (roughly 5–7 μm) are the workhorses for semi-matte and matte wood and general industrial coatings.
  • Coarse grades (roughly 8 μm and above) reach very low gloss and suit thicker films or textured, deep-matte plastic finishes, at the cost of a rougher feel and lower clarity.

A practical rule used by many formulators is to keep the d50 below the dry film thickness and the top size close to it. If your plastic coating dries to 10–15 μm, a coarse grade will usually poke through; if your floor finish is 40 μm, a very fine grade may need an uneconomical dosage. Compare particle size data only when measured by the same method.

Parameter 2: pore volume and oil absorption

Pore volume is the internal void space of the silica particle, usually reported in mL/g. High pore volume means a lighter, more voluminous particle: more particles per gram, and therefore more surface roughness per percent added. That is why pore volume is one of the best predictors of matting efficiency.

Oil absorption (commonly measured to a method such as ISO 787-5) is a related indicator: highly porous grades absorb more oil and also more binder and water. The trade-off is that high pore volume grades tend to raise viscosity more and can make the film more sensitive to burnishing if the particles are fragile. When you compare two grades, look at pore volume, oil absorption and particle size together. Two grades with the same d50 but different pore volumes will not give the same gloss at the same dosage.

Parameter 3: surface treatment (wax-treated vs untreated)

Many silica matting agents for waterborne systems are surface treated, most often with a polyethylene or other wax. The treatment changes behaviour in several useful ways:

  • Soft, stir-back sediment. Untreated silica can pack and bond into a hard cake during storage, especially in low-viscosity systems kept warm. A wax layer keeps particles apart so the sediment redisperses with stirring. Our article on eliminating hard sediment covers the mechanism.
  • Scratch resistance and feel. Wax at the surface tends to improve mar resistance and gives a smoother hand.
  • Reduced interaction with the aqueous phase. Treated surfaces generally interact less with water and thickeners than bare silanol surfaces.

The downsides to watch: some treated grades matte slightly less efficiently than untreated ones of the same size, and excessive wax can affect intercoat adhesion or recoatability. Check recoat adhesion with a cross-cut test if the coating is a sealer or is sanded and overcoated.

Parameter 4: effect on viscosity and rheology

Every matting agent thickens a waterborne coating to some degree, because the porous particles absorb part of the liquid phase and add surface area. Surface area for typical precipitated matting silicas is in the low hundreds of m²/g, and the combination of surface area and pore volume largely sets how much the viscosity rises. The practical questions are how much, and whether it stays stable.

  • Measure viscosity before and after adding the matting agent, and again after 24 hours, because porous silica can keep absorbing liquid after dispersion.
  • Check the low-shear viscosity as well as the application viscosity. Associative thickeners and silica can interact, changing sag resistance and levelling.
  • If a new grade raises viscosity more than your current one, you may need to adjust water or thickener, which turns a simple swap into a reformulation.

Parameter 5: clarity and refractive index

For clear coats on wood, clarity is often as important as gloss. Amorphous silica has a refractive index of about 1.46, close to (but not identical to) common acrylic and polyurethane binders. The closer the match and the better the dispersion, the less light the particles scatter inside the film, and the less milky or grey the coating looks on dark wood.

Clarity is affected by particle size (finer is usually clearer), the share of fines and agglomerates, the dosage and the surface treatment. The most useful test is simple: apply the matted clear coat over a black glass panel or a dark stained board next to your reference, at the same gloss, and compare the depth of black. Also check panels dried at high humidity, because some waterborne matte films whiten when they dry slowly.

Parameter 6: dispersibility

Silica matting agents are designed to be dispersed, not ground. Over-grinding breaks the porous particles, reduces matting efficiency and can raise gloss. Most modern grades for water based coatings disperse with a high-speed disperser; bead milling is usually unnecessary and often counterproductive.

  • Add the powder slowly into the vortex at moderate speed, then increase to full dispersion speed.
  • Run long enough for a consistent Hegman reading. Under-dispersion leaves agglomerates (seeds and uneven gloss); over-dispersion fractures particles.
  • Record tip speed, time and batch temperature, so the lab result can be reproduced in production.

Parameter 7: storage stability and batch consistency

The last parameter is what happens after the batch leaves the disperser. Check settling and redispersion after accelerated storage (for example, a few weeks at elevated temperature such as 40–50 °C, as many labs do), viscosity drift, gloss drift on panels drawn from stored paint, and pH. Then ask the supplier how consistent the grade is from lot to lot: a COA that reports particle size, pore volume or oil absorption, moisture and pH for every batch lets you set incoming QC limits.

A lab checklist for selecting a matting agent for water based coatings

  1. Define the target: binder type, dry film thickness, gloss target at 60° (and 85° for very low gloss), substrate colour and required feel.
  2. Shortlist two or three grades whose d50 fits the film thickness and whose pore volume suits the gloss target.
  3. Build a ladder series (for example 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% on total formulation) for each grade and your current reference.
  4. Disperse under identical conditions and record Hegman, viscosity at 0 h and 24 h, and pH.
  5. Draw down at your real film thickness; measure gloss (ISO 2813 / ASTM D523) after full cure, and compare clarity on a dark substrate.
  6. Run accelerated storage, then check sediment, viscosity and gloss on panels made from stored paint.
  7. Do a production-scale trial before approving, and set incoming QC limits from the supplier's COA parameters.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is the best matting agent for water based coatings?

There is no single best grade. Amorphous precipitated silica, often wax treated, is the most common choice. Select it by matching particle size to dry film thickness, pore volume to the gloss target, and surface treatment to your storage and scratch requirements, then confirm with a ladder test in your own formulation.

2. How much matting agent should I add to a waterborne coating?

Silica matting agents are typically dosed at a few percent of the total formulation; for Censilcoat grades the recommended range is 1–4%. The exact level depends on the gloss target, film thickness, binder and grade, so build a ladder series and measure gloss after full cure.

3. Do wax-treated matting agents affect recoat adhesion?

They can if the wax level is high or the coating is a sealer that will be sanded and overcoated. Run a cross-cut adhesion test on the recoated system before approving a wax-treated grade for multi-coat use.

4. Why does my waterborne matte clear coat look grey on dark wood?

A grey or milky cast usually comes from light scattering by coarse particles, agglomerates or excess dosage, and can be made worse by slow drying in high humidity. A finer, well-dispersed, high-clarity grade at the lowest effective dosage usually helps.

5. Do I need a bead mill to disperse silica matting agents?

Usually not. Most matting silicas for waterborne coatings are designed for high-speed dispersers, and bead milling can fracture the porous particles and raise gloss. Censilcoat grades disperse with high-speed stirring in 15–20 minutes.

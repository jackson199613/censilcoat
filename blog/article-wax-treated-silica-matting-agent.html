Wax-Treated vs Untreated Silica Matting Agents: When the Wax Coating Matters

Published: 2026-11-24 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Core product

A wax treated silica matting agent is a precipitated (or gel) silica whose particles carry a thin organic coating, usually a polyethylene or other synthetic wax, applied during micronisation. Untreated grades are the same porous silica without that coating. The choice is not cosmetic: the wax layer changes how the silica settles in the can, how the dried film feels and resists scratching, how it recoats, and how much silica you need to hit a gloss target. This guide explains what the wax actually does, where it helps, where it causes trouble, and how to decide between wax-treated and untreated grades with a short set of lab tests.

It is written for formulators of waterborne wood and plastic coatings, and for the buyers who need to know whether two grades with the same particle size are really interchangeable.

What a wax treated silica matting agent is

Matting silica is made by precipitating amorphous silicon dioxide, washing, drying and milling it to a controlled particle size. Surface treatment is usually done at the milling stage: wax is fed into the jet or classifier mill with the silica, melts or softens in the hot air stream, and spreads over the particle surface as the particles are reduced to their final size. Some producers instead use polymeric or other organic treatments rather than wax; these behave differently again, and this article focuses on wax.

Three variables define a treated grade:

  • Wax type. Polyethylene waxes are common. Their melting point matters: a high-melt wax survives forced drying and warm storage without softening, migrating or smearing, while a low-melt wax can move toward the film surface when the coating is heated.
  • Wax level. Treatment levels vary widely between producers and grades, from a few percent up to well over 10% of the particle weight. Datasheets often show it indirectly as carbon content.
  • Coverage. The wax sits mostly on the outer surface and pore openings. It does not fill the internal pore volume, which is why a treated grade still matts efficiently.

The underlying silica (particle size distribution, pore volume, surface area) still does most of the matting work. The wax changes how the particles interact with each other, with the binder and with whatever touches the cured film.

What the wax coating changes

1. Settling behaviour in the can

This is the main reason wax-treated grades dominate low-viscosity waterborne systems. Untreated silica carries a dense layer of surface silanol (Si–OH) groups. When particles settle and come into contact, those groups can form hydrogen bonds between particles, and the sediment packs into a hard cake that a paddle will not lift. A wax layer acts as a spacer: it reduces direct silica-to-silica contact, so the sediment stays loose and stirs back into suspension. Wax does not stop settling (gravity still wins in a thin base), but it changes hard settling into soft settling. Our article on eliminating hard sediment in waterborne matte coatings covers the mechanism and the rheology side in more detail.

2. Surface feel, mar and burnish resistance

Silica particles that protrude at the surface of a matte film are what you feel with your fingertips and what a fingernail, cloth or coin rubs against. A wax shell lowers friction at those contact points, which generally gives a smoother, silkier feel, fewer fingernail ("writing") marks and less polishing when the surface is rubbed. That is why wax-treated grades are the usual choice for furniture, cabinets and flooring that will be handled daily. See our guide to burnishing in matte coatings for how to test and fix polishing marks.

3. Matting efficiency

Weight for weight, a heavily treated grade contains less silica than an untreated grade, and the wax can slightly change how particles arrange during film shrinkage. In practice that often means a modest increase in dosage to reach the same gloss, though grades are designed differently and the only reliable answer comes from a dosage ladder in your own base.

4. Wetting and incorporation

Wax makes the surface more hydrophobic. In a waterborne mill base, a treated grade can float on the surface for longer during addition and may need a suitable wetting agent and adequate disperser speed to draw it down. Once wetted, wax-treated grades generally disperse easily because the particles are already at their final size; the disperser only needs to break up loose agglomerates.

5. Intercoat adhesion and surface effects

The same low surface energy that helps mar resistance can work against you. Too much wax, a poorly matched wax, or a low-melt wax that migrates can reduce the adhesion of the next coat, especially if the first coat is not sanded. In some systems free or loosely bound wax can contribute to cratering or affect slip. These are not reasons to avoid treated grades, but they are reasons to test recoat adhesion before approving one.

6. Clarity

Wax has a refractive index close to many binders, and treatment itself is not usually the main driver of haze; particle size distribution, top size and dosage matter more. A poorly dispersed or over-treated grade can still add a faint milkiness over dark wood, so clarity should be checked on a black panel rather than assumed.

When the wax coating matters, and when it doesn't

Choose a wax-treated grade when:

  • The base is thin. Waterborne acrylic and PUD topcoats at low viscosity give silica little support during storage and transport. Soft settling is often the difference between a product that ships and one that comes back.
  • The film will be touched. Tabletops, cabinet doors, chair arms, floors and plastic housings all benefit from lower friction at the surface.
  • It is the final coat. Topcoats are rarely recoated after cure, so the adhesion downside matters less.
  • Feel is part of the specification. Smooth or tactile matte finishes depend partly on the particle surface.

Consider an untreated (or lightly treated) grade when:

  • The coat will be recoated. In sanding sealers and primers, intercoat adhesion usually outranks surface slip. Many formulators keep sealers lightly matted or unmatted for this reason.
  • The formulation already controls settling. A system with a robust thixotropic structure can carry untreated silica without hard caking.
  • Every gram of matting efficiency counts. In highly filled or very thin films where you are already near the viscosity limit, the slightly higher efficiency of an untreated grade can help.
  • Wax interferes with a downstream step. Printing, laminating, bonding or tape adhesion on the coated surface can be sensitive to low surface energy.

Many production formulas are compromises: a wax-treated grade in the topcoat, little or no silica in the sealer, and sometimes a blend of treated and untreated silica tuned for feel and recoat window.

A lab protocol to compare treated and untreated grades

When you evaluate a wax-treated silica matting agent against an untreated one, or against your incumbent grade, keep every variable fixed except the silica. The following tests answer most questions in about two weeks.

  1. Gloss vs dosage. Prepare three or four dosage levels of each grade with identical dispersion time and speed. Measure 60° and 85° gloss (ISO 2813 or ASTM D523) at a fixed dry film thickness, and plot the curves to find equal-gloss dosages.
  2. Storage and settling. Store sealed samples at room temperature and under heat ageing (7 days at 50 °C is a common screening condition). Probe the sediment with a spatula, rate it as soft or hard, and check how easily it stirs back. Re-measure viscosity and gloss afterwards.
  3. Feel and mar. Compare panels by touch with a small blind panel of colleagues, then rub with a fingernail, a coin and a dry cloth. Measure gloss before and after a defined number of rubs to quantify burnishing.
  4. Recoat adhesion. Apply a second coat over the first, both sanded and unsanded, after the realistic recoat interval. Run cross-cut adhesion (ISO 2409 or ASTM D3359) and look for crawling or cratering.
  5. Clarity. Draw both formulations down over a black panel and over the darkest stain your customers use, and compare side by side under consistent lighting.

Compare at equal gloss, not equal dosage. A treated grade that needs 0.3% more silica but passes storage and mar tests may still be the cheaper option once returns and rework are counted.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is a wax-treated silica matting agent?

It is a precipitated or gel silica whose particles carry a thin wax coating, usually polyethylene wax, applied during milling. The silica still does the matting; the wax changes settling, surface feel, mar resistance and recoat behaviour.

2. Does wax treatment stop silica from settling?

No. It changes hard settling into soft settling. Wax reduces direct particle-to-particle contact, so the sediment stays loose and stirs back. Rheology additives are still needed if you want to reduce how much settles.

3. Are wax-treated grades less efficient at matting?

Often slightly, because part of the particle weight is wax. The difference depends on the grade, so compare grades at equal gloss with a dosage ladder in your own base rather than at equal dosage.

4. Can wax-treated silica cause recoat adhesion problems?

It can, particularly with high wax levels, low-melt waxes or unsanded recoating. Run cross-cut adhesion on sanded and unsanded recoats before approving a grade for a coat that will be overcoated.

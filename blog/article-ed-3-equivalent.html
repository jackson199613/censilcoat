ED 3 Equivalent: A Universal Fine Matting Grade

Published: 2026-11-27 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Sourcing

An ED 3 equivalent is a fine, general-purpose silica matting agent that can take the place of ED 3 in an existing wood or industrial coating, delivering the same gloss level, a clean surface and stable cans, without a new formulation project. Finding one is less about copying a single number from a datasheet and more about deciding which properties your coating actually depends on: particle size, the width of the size distribution, porosity and surface treatment. Then you confirm the swap with a short side-by-side lab trial in your own formula.

This guide covers what ED 3 is published to be, which properties a substitute must match, the trade-off between a finer grade and a closer particle-size match, a lab plan for qualifying the replacement, and how to correct dosage without touching the rest of the formulation.

What ED 3 is and why formulators use it

ED 3 belongs to a family of precipitated silica matting agents that has been a reference point in wood and industrial coatings for many years. Within that family it sits in the middle: finer than the coarse, high-efficiency grades used for deep matte, but coarser than the grades chosen for maximum smoothness. That position is exactly why formulators treat it as a "universal" fine grade. It mats efficiently enough for semi-matte and matte finishes, yet it is fine enough to keep the surface even and the film reasonably transparent.

According to the product properties table its supplier publishes for the EMEA region, ED 3 has an average particle size of about 6.0 μm (light scattering), no surface treatment, a pH of around 7 and an oil absorption of about 320 g/100 g. Pore volume and BET surface area are not stated in that table. We reproduce these figures, with the source cited, on our ED 3 alternative page.

Two points from that data shape the whole substitution exercise. First, ED 3 is untreated, so it carries no wax or organic layer. Second, its published size is an average from one measuring method, which may not compare directly with a d50 reported by another supplier on another instrument.

Properties an ED 3 equivalent has to match

A credible equivalent has to land close to ED 3 on the properties that control appearance and processing. In order of importance for most coatings:

  • Median particle size (d50). The d50 sets the scale of surface roughness the silica produces as the film shrinks, so it governs how fast gloss drops with dosage and how fine or coarse the surface looks and feels. For a fine universal grade, the useful band is roughly 4.5–6.5 μm.
  • Distribution width. The coarse tail (d90 and above) decides whether a thin film shows seeds, grit or a grey cast. The fine end adds viscosity and surface area without adding much matting. Two grades with the same d50 can behave differently if one has a broader spread.
  • Pore volume. Porous silica delivers more particles per gram, which raises matting efficiency. It also changes how much liquid the powder takes up, which affects viscosity after letdown.
  • Surface treatment. Treated versus untreated is a deliberate design choice, not a detail. It affects settling, surface slip and feel, burnish resistance and, in waterborne systems, the tendency to whiten at high humidity.
  • Secondary checks. pH, moisture, oil absorption and SiO₂ content are consistency and compatibility checks. They rarely decide the outcome on their own, but they belong on the batch COA.

If a figure is missing from either datasheet, treat it as unknown rather than matching. Request the value from the supplier or measure both grades with the same method.

Finer grade or closer size match? Making the trade-off

A substitute rarely matches every property exactly, so the practical question is which direction of difference your coating can tolerate. The table compares the options in general terms.

For many plants the treated, slightly finer option is not a compromise but an upgrade: they keep the gloss target with a small dosage tweak and gain a smoother surface and softer sediment. For others, any visible change in feel triggers a customer re-approval, and the closest particle-size match is the safer route. Decide which case you are in before ordering samples. A lab plan to qualify an ED 3 equivalent

Run the candidate against ED 3 in your production formulation, with everything except the matting agent held constant. A compact protocol:

  1. Record the baseline. Note the current dosage, dispersion settings, viscosity, fineness of grind, gloss at 60° and 85°, and film thickness from a recent production batch.
  2. Disperse both grades identically. Same order of addition, disperser speed and time. Check fineness of grind with a Hegman gauge and record viscosity right after dispersion and again after 24 hours. A fine grade should give a consistent reading with no stray specks beyond the main band.
  3. Build a gloss curve. Prepare a dosage ladder for each grade, for example the current dosage plus and minus 0.5%. Draw down or spray at production film build, cure under the same conditions, and measure 60° and 85° gloss (ISO 2813 or ASTM D523). The curve tells you whether the candidate is more or less efficient and by how much.
  4. Compare surface and clarity. Inspect panels side by side over a dark substrate for haze, seeds and grey cast, and assess touch blind if feel matters to your customers. Check burnish resistance by rubbing a defined area and re-measuring gloss.
  5. Test stability. Store both batches at elevated temperature (7 days at 50 °C is a common accelerated check), then rate sediment hardness, re-stir effort, viscosity change and any gloss drift on fresh panels.
  6. Confirm on the line. When the bench results pass, run one production-scale batch and compare against retained panels from the last ED 3 batch.

Typical pass criteria: gloss within your specification tolerance at the same dosage or after a small correction, no visible difference in haze or seeds, feel equal or better, and sediment no harder than the control. Correcting dosage without reformulating

If the candidate lands a few gloss units away from the target at equal dosage, the fix is almost always a small dosage change, not a new formula. Read the required dosage off the two gloss curves rather than guessing. Adjustments within about ±0.3% on total formulation are normally absorbed without visible side effects, and that is the range we recommend fine-tuning within.

Some practical rules help keep the correction clean:

  • Check both angles. A finer grade can match 60° gloss but read differently at 85°, which is what observers see as sheen at low viewing angles. Set targets for both if your specification allows.
  • Watch viscosity as you add. Extra silica raises viscosity. If application viscosity moves outside the window, adjust with water or the thickener you already use, not with a new additive.
  • Keep film build constant. Gloss in matte coatings moves with dry film thickness. A dosage correction measured on thinner panels than production will mislead you.
  • Stop if you need far more. If the candidate needs well over a full percentage point more than ED 3, it is the wrong grade for the job. Choose a coarser or more efficient grade rather than over-dosing, which costs clarity, viscosity and money.

What procurement should confirm before switching

Once R&D approves the candidate, the supply side needs the same rigour. Agree on the parameters printed on every batch COA (at least d50, pH and moisture) and their accepted ranges, and ask for data from more than one production lot. Collect the current TDS and SDS, and confirm any statements your export customers require. Clarify MOQ, lead time to your port, bag size and storage conditions, because a change from one bag size to another affects batch tickets and warehouse handling. Finally, agree on how the supplier will notify process or raw-material changes. Many plants keep ED 3 as an approved grade and run the equivalent as a qualified second source. This gives them a fallback when import lead times stretch or prices move.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Is Censil351C a 1:1 ED 3 equivalent?

It is benchmarked against ED 3 for a 1:1 trial at your current dosage. Its d50 of 4.8–5.1 μm is finer than the 6.0 μm average published for ED 3, and it is wax-modified, so confirm gloss in your own system and fine-tune dosage within ±0.3% if needed.

2. Why is ED 3 described as a universal fine grade?

It sits in the middle of its family: fine enough to keep the surface smooth and the film reasonably transparent, yet efficient enough for semi-matte and matte finishes. That balance makes it usable across many wood and industrial coatings.

3. Does it matter that ED 3 is untreated and the substitute is wax-treated?

Usually it helps in waterborne systems, where a wax layer gives softer sediment and a smoother feel. Check recoat adhesion and gloss in your trial, because the treatment can shift results slightly in some formulations.

4. What should I do if the equivalent gives higher gloss at the same dosage?

Read the required dosage off a gloss curve and add in small steps, typically within ±0.3%. If you need much more than ED 3 to reach target, choose a coarser or more efficient grade instead of over-dosing.

5. How long does it take to qualify an ED 3 equivalent?

A bench comparison of dispersion, gloss curve, surface and accelerated storage usually takes one to two weeks, followed by one production-scale batch before full approval.

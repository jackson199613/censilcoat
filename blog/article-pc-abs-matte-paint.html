Matte Paints for PC/ABS Electronics Housings

Published: 2026-11-25 · Censilcoat Technical Team · Plastic coatings

A good PC/ABS matte paint has to do several things at once on an electronics housing: hit a tight low-gloss target, feel fine and even in the hand, survive sweat, hand cream, alcohol wipes and daily abrasion, and do all of that in a thin film on a substrate that is sensitive to solvents and moulded-in stress. The matting agent sits at the centre of that balance. This guide explains how PC/ABS behaves under a coating, which binder systems are common, how to choose and dose a silica matting agent for thin 3C films, and which lab tests catch problems before a housing reaches the assembly line.

Why PC/ABS housings are demanding to paint

PC/ABS is a blend of polycarbonate and acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene. It is popular for laptop shells, chargers, router and set-top box enclosures, remote controls and wearable parts because it combines the impact strength and heat resistance of PC with the easier moulding of ABS. For the coating formulator, the blend brings a few specific risks:

  • Environmental stress cracking. Polycarbonate is prone to crazing and cracking when solvents meet moulded-in stress, especially near gates, bosses, snap fits and screw holes. Aggressive co-solvents or high coalescent levels can trigger cracks that only appear days later or after thermal cycling.
  • Low surface energy and mould release. Residual release agents, silicone contamination and handling oils cause poor wetting, cratering and adhesion loss. A matte film hides small defects visually but not adhesion failures.
  • Limited bake temperature. Parts are usually force-dried at moderate temperatures to avoid warping. That limits how far the film can be cured and makes coalescence and drying conditions more important for final gloss.
  • Thin films with tight tolerances. Electronics topcoats are often applied at low dry film thickness, commonly somewhere in the 10–25 μm range depending on the build-up. In thin films, matting particle size, top size and dosage have a large and visible effect on gloss, touch and specks.

Many of the same principles apply to plain ABS; our article on matte waterborne coatings for ABS parts covers surface preparation in more detail.

Binder systems used for PC/ABS matte paint

The binder decides adhesion, chemical resistance and hardness; the matting agent then has to work inside that film. The main families used on PC/ABS electronics housings are summarised below as general industry practice.

SystemTypical roleMatting considerations
1K waterborne acrylic or acrylic-PU hybridCost-sensitive housings, primers, single-coat finishesEasy to mat; check resistance to alcohol and hand cream, which can be weaker than 2K
2K waterborne PU (polyol + polyisocyanate)Durable topcoats for frequently handled devicesSilica goes in the polyol component; check gloss across pot life and for CO2 microfoam

Whatever the system, check its co-solvent package on stressed PC/ABS parts before you optimise gloss. A beautiful matte finish that cracks the housing near a screw boss is a failed formulation.

Choosing the matting agent for PC/ABS matte paint

Amorphous precipitated silica, usually wax-treated for waterborne use, is the standard matting agent in this market. When comparing grades for electronics housings, focus on these parameters:

  • d50 relative to dry film thickness. In a 15 μm film, a 6 μm d50 grade gives efficient matting with a fine feel. Much coarser grades can protrude too far, giving a dry, sandy touch and visible specks, while much finer grades can sink and matt poorly in thicker builds.
  • Top size and distribution width. On a dark housing under shop lighting, a few oversized particles show up as sparkle or seeds. A narrow particle size distribution matters more than the d50 alone.
  • Pore volume. Higher pore volume means more particles per gram and more matting per unit of dosage, which leaves more binder to carry chemical and abrasion resistance.
  • Surface treatment. Wax treatment helps prevent hard sediment in low-viscosity waterborne paints and contributes to a smoother hand and better mar resistance. It can affect recoat adhesion, so check intercoat adhesion if the matte layer is overcoated.
  • Effect on viscosity. High-surface-area silica thickens the paint. In spray-applied 3C coatings at low solids, a grade that spikes viscosity forces extra water, changes atomisation and shifts gloss.

Gloss targets, film build and dosage

Electronics brands specify gloss in a narrow band, often in the low single digits to low teens at 60°, and the tolerance can be just a few gloss units. At these levels, 60° readings bunch together, so many labs also record 85° gloss, which separates samples that look the same at 60° but differ visibly at grazing angles. Gloss should be measured per ISO 2813 or ASTM D523 on panels of known dry film thickness.

Building a dosage ladder

  1. Fix the substrate (moulded PC/ABS plaques from the production resin, not generic panels), cleaning method, primer and target DFT.
  2. Prepare the base paint without matting agent and record its gloss.
  3. Add silica at 1%, 2%, 3% and 4% on total formulation, dispersing each step the same way.
  4. Spray at the production DFT, dry under the line schedule, and condition before measuring 60° and 85° gloss.
  5. Plot gloss against dosage and pick the dosage on the flat part of the curve, where small weighing or DFT variations change gloss least.

If a grade needs well over 4–5% to reach the target, it is usually better to choose a more efficient grade than to overload the film, because high silica levels raise viscosity and can reduce flexibility, chemical resistance and adhesion.

Film thickness sensitivity

Thin matte films often show gloss rising or falling with DFT. Spray panels at the lower and upper limits of the line tolerance as well as at nominal. If gloss moves outside specification across that window, the formulation will produce visible differences between the flat face and the edges or recesses of a housing.

Dispersion and spray application tips

Matting silicas are supplied at their final particle size, so high-speed dispersion only needs to wet the powder and break loose agglomerates. Add the silica slowly into the vortex after the defoamer and wetting agent, disperse at high speed, then add thickener and surface additives last. Grinding harder does not improve matting and can warm the batch, entrain air and break structure in the binder. A Hegman gauge reading after let-down confirms that no coarse agglomerates remain.

On the spray line, keep these practical points in mind:

  • Filter the paint before use to remove dried skin and agglomerates, which show up as seeds on dark housings.
  • Keep atomisation pressure, fluid flow and gun distance constant; changes alter how much the film shrinks and how particles orient, which changes gloss.
  • Control flash-off and drying temperature and airflow. A fast-skinning surface traps particles differently than slow, even drying.
  • Stir or recirculate the paint during use so silica does not settle in lines or cups, which causes gloss drift through a shift.

Lab tests for matte electronics housings

Brand-owner specifications vary, so use the customer's own test method where one exists. As a general industry checklist, matte PC/ABS coatings are commonly checked for:

PropertyTypical methodWhat it reveals about matting
Gloss at 60° and 85°ISO 2813 / ASTM D523Matting efficiency and uniformity across DFT
AdhesionCross-cut per ISO 2409 / ASTM D3359, before and after humidity exposureWhether high silica loading or wax treatment weakens adhesion
Pencil hardnessISO 15184 / ASTM D3363Film hardness with silica present
Abrasion and polishingPaper-tape abrasion tester (often called RCA), eraser or cloth rubBurnishing: shiny spots where particles are flattened or pulled out
Chemical resistanceAlcohol rub, artificial sweat, hand cream or sunscreen contactSoftening or gloss change around particles
Environmental durabilityHumidity, heat and thermal cycling exposureCracking on the PC/ABS substrate, whitening, gloss shift
AppearanceVisual check under standard lighting, on dark coloursSeeds, sparkle, patchy sheen, grey cast

Measure gloss before and after abrasion and chemical exposure. A matte coating that passes adhesion and hardness but goes from 5 GU to 12 GU after rubbing will still generate customer complaints, because users see shiny thumb areas on a device long before they notice a scratch.

Common problems and fixes

  • Polished thumb or palm areas: the binder is too soft or the silica is not well anchored. Try a wax-treated grade, a harder binder or crosslinker, and avoid overdosing.
  • Sandy or dry feel: particle size is too coarse for the film thickness. Move to a finer grade or increase DFT slightly, or add a slip additive.
  • Seeds and sparkle on black housings: agglomerates or oversized particles. Check dispersion and filtration, and look at grade top size.
  • Gloss differs between parts in one batch: DFT variation across complex geometry or settling in the feed system. Tighten spray parameters and keep paint agitated.
  • Cracks near bosses after drying or cycling: stress cracking from co-solvents. Revisit the solvent package and annealing of parts, not the matting agent.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What gloss level is typical for PC/ABS matte paint on electronics?

Brand specifications vary, but matte electronics housings are often specified in the low single digits to low teens at 60°, with tight tolerances. Record 85° gloss as well, because it separates samples that read the same at 60°.

2. Which matting agent particle size suits thin 3C topcoats?

Choose the d50 relative to dry film thickness. In films of roughly 10–25 μm, fine grades around 5–7 μm d50 with a narrow distribution give efficient matting and a fine feel; much coarser grades can feel sandy and show specks.

3. Why do matte PC/ABS housings turn shiny where people hold them?

This is burnishing: abrasion flattens or pulls out surface particles. A harder or crosslinked binder, wax-treated silica and avoiding overdosing help. Measure gloss before and after an abrasion or rub test.

4. Can the matting agent cause stress cracking on PC/ABS?

Stress cracking is normally caused by co-solvents and moulded-in stress, not by the silica. Test the full paint on stressed production parts and adjust the solvent package if cracks appear near bosses or gates.

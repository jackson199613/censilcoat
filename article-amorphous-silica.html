What Is Amorphous Silica? Structure, Types and Why It Matters in Coatings

Amorphous and crystalline silica share one formula but behave very differently. How the structure drives transparency, matting efficiency and safety classification — and what to check on a datasheet.

Published November 9, 2026 · Censilcoat technical team

Quick answer

What is amorphous silica?

Amorphous silica is silicon dioxide (SiO₂) whose atoms form a random network with no long-range crystal order. Synthetic amorphous silica — precipitated, gel, fumed and colloidal — is the standard additive in coatings because it is porous, nearly index-matched to clear binders, chemically inert and easy to surface-treat. Unlike respirable crystalline silica, it is not classified as a human carcinogen (IARC Group 3) and is generally not classified as hazardous under GHS, though it should still be handled as a fine dust.

Almost every matting agent, anti-settling additive and free-flow aid in a modern coating plant is some form of silicon dioxide. Yet "silica" covers two materials that behave very differently: crystalline silica, the mineral in quartz sand, and amorphous silica, the engineered powder that goes into your can. For formulators and buyers the distinction is not academic. It decides how transparent a matte clear looks, how the powder is classified on the safety data sheet, and which numbers on a datasheet actually matter.

What "amorphous" actually means

Both forms are built from the same unit: a silicon atom bonded to four oxygen atoms in a tetrahedron. The difference is how those tetrahedra connect.

  • Crystalline silica (quartz, cristobalite, tridymite) stacks the tetrahedra in a repeating lattice with long-range order. X-ray diffraction shows sharp, characteristic peaks.
  • Amorphous silica links the same tetrahedra in a random three-dimensional network with no long-range order — closer to a frozen liquid than a crystal. X-ray diffraction shows only a broad, featureless hump.

Because there is no lattice to cleave along, amorphous silica can be built as porous particles with very high internal surface area. That open, disordered structure is exactly what makes it useful as a functional additive — and what separates it from ground quartz used as a filler.

Natural vs. synthetic amorphous silica

Amorphous silica occurs in nature (diatomaceous earth, opal, volcanic glass, rice-husk ash), but natural sources often contain or convert into crystalline phases, especially when calcined. For coatings, the material of choice is synthetic amorphous silica (SAS): made from a chemical process, controlled batch by batch, and free of detectable crystalline silica.

SAS comes in four main families, each produced by a different route:

TypeHow it is madeStructureTypical coating use
Precipitated silicaSodium silicate + acid under controlled pH and temperature; filtered, washed, dried and milledAggregated particles, broad pore rangeMatting agents, anti-settling, reinforcement
Silica gelSodium silicate + acid forming a continuous gel, then washed, aged and milledRigid particles with a defined internal pore systemMatting agents (especially high-clarity), desiccants
Fumed (pyrogenic) silicaFlame hydrolysis of silicon tetrachloride in a hydrogen–oxygen flameNon-porous nano-sized primary particles fused into chain-like aggregatesThixotropy, rheology control, anti-settling
Colloidal silica (silica sol)Growth of discrete particles in a stabilised aqueous dispersionDense, spherical nanoparticles in liquidHardness, scratch and block resistance, binders

All four are amorphous. What differs is particle size, porosity and surface chemistry — and those are what you are really buying.

Why coatings rely on amorphous silica

1. Transparency in clear films

Amorphous silica has a refractive index of about 1.45–1.46, close to that of common acrylic, polyurethane and UV-curable binders (roughly 1.47–1.50). When the particle and the cured resin bend light almost equally, the particle scatters little light inside the film, so a matte clear stays clear. Quartz sits higher, around 1.54–1.55, which is one reason ground crystalline fillers turn clear coats cloudy. On dark solid wood this difference is the line between "water-white matte" and "milky matte."

2. Porosity drives matting efficiency

A matting agent works by creating micron-scale roughness at the very top of the dried film. Porous amorphous particles are light (low tapped density) and voluminous, so each gram supplies many particles and a lot of surface texture. That is why coating-grade silicas are specified by pore volume (typically 1.6–1.8 mL/g for matting grades) and BET surface area (often 250–350 m²/g), not just by particle size. More pore volume means more matting per gram — and more binder absorbed, which is the viscosity trade-off covered in our matting-agent selection guide.

3. A reactive, tunable surface

The surface of amorphous silica is covered with silanol (Si–OH) groups. They make the particle hydrophilic and allow it to form hydrogen-bonded networks — useful for fumed-silica thixotropy, but also the root cause of hard sediment when bare particles pack together in low-viscosity waterborne paints. Because silanols are reactive, the surface can be modified with wax or organic treatments to soften settling, improve touch and reduce water pickup at high humidity. We explain the mechanism in eliminating hard sediment in waterborne matte coatings.

4. Chemical inertness and purity

High-purity SAS (typically ≥ 99% SiO₂ on a dry basis) is chemically inert in coating formulations, colourless and stable to UV and heat. It does not yellow, react with the binder or catalyse side reactions — a quiet but important property in clear and light-coloured finishes.

Safety and regulatory status: why "amorphous" matters on the SDS

The health picture of the two forms is very different, and this is the question procurement and EHS teams ask most often.

  • Respirable crystalline silica is a recognised occupational hazard. It can cause silicosis, and IARC classifies it as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1). In the United States, OSHA limits respirable crystalline silica to 50 µg/m³ as an 8-hour time-weighted average.
  • Synthetic amorphous silica is treated differently. IARC places amorphous silica in Group 3 (not classifiable as to carcinogenicity in humans), and SAS is generally not classified as hazardous under GHS/EU CLP. Inhaled amorphous particles are cleared from the lungs and dissolve slowly rather than persisting like crystalline particles.

"Not classified" is not the same as "no precautions." Any fine powder is a nuisance dust: use local exhaust ventilation when charging dispersers, wear suitable dust masks and eye protection, and follow your local occupational exposure limits for inhalable and respirable dust. Always work from the supplier's current SDS for the specific grade.

When you qualify a supplier, ask for evidence that the grade is free of crystalline silica — typically an X-ray diffraction result showing no crystalline peaks above the detection limit. Synthetic amorphous silicas are commonly registered under CAS numbers such as 7631-86-9 or 112926-00-8 (silica gel and precipitated silica, crystalline-free); fumed silica is often listed as 112945-52-5. The CAS number tells you the family; the XRD result tells you the batch is clean.

Key takeaways

  • Amorphous and crystalline silica share a chemical formula but not a structure — and that structure drives both performance and safety classification.
  • Synthetic amorphous silica (precipitated, gel, fumed, colloidal) is the standard in coatings because it is porous, transparent in clear films, inert and tunable at the surface.
  • Refractive-index matching with the binder is why amorphous silica keeps matte clears transparent.
  • Respirable crystalline silica is a Group 1 carcinogen; synthetic amorphous silica is IARC Group 3 and generally not classified as hazardous — but always handle it as a fine dust.
  • Ask suppliers for XRD evidence of crystalline-free material, then compare grades on particle size, pore volume, surface area and surface treatment.
